### Part 2: Detailed Explanations

## Topic 5.1: The Enlightenment

**Question 1: (C) — John Locke and Natural Rights**  
Answer: The Declaration of Independence (1776).  
Why: Jefferson took the phrase "unalienable rights" from Locke and used the right to revolt to justify independence. Leviathan argued the opposite (surrender rights to a strong ruler), and the Congress of Vienna restored monarchies rather than overthrowing them.

**Question 2: (D) — Thomas Hobbes's Leviathan**  
Answer: A strong central authority that keeps order, even at some cost to rights.  
Why: Hobbes's social contract trades rights for security, which justified strong rule. The right of revolution is Locke's idea, separation of powers is Montesquieu's, and laissez-faire is Adam Smith's.

**Question 3: (B) — Montesquieu and the Separation of Powers**  
Answer: Enlightenment political theory shaped the structure of new constitutional governments.  
Why: Montesquieu's separation of powers became the framework of the U.S. government, showing ideas turning into institutions. The passage is about praise for limited, checked power, not absolutism, conservatism, or mercantilism.

**Question 4: (A) — Rousseau and the General Will**  
Answer: The belief that monarchs ruled by divine right, not the people's will.  
Why: If the sovereign must carry out the people's General Will, power comes from the people, not from God or birth. Natural rights, nationalism, and empiricism were all compatible with or encouraged by Enlightenment thought.

**Question 5: (A) — Adam Smith and Mercantilism**  
Answer: A shift away from government-regulated trade toward laissez-faire capitalism.  
Why: Smith opposed mercantilist controls and favored minimal government intervention, which became laissez-faire capitalism. State ownership and communal ownership are socialist ideas, and higher tariffs are mercantilist.

**Question 6: (D) — Deism and Thomas Paine**  
Answer: Applying reason and scientific thinking to questions of religion.  
Why: Deists explained God through natural laws understood by science, reflecting the Enlightenment's emphasis on reason. Deism weakened, rather than strengthened, church authority and built on the Scientific Revolution rather than rejecting it.

**Question 7: (C) — Feminism and the Rights of Man**  
Answer: Excluded groups used Enlightenment ideals to demand inclusion.  
Why: Both writers turned the language of natural rights against the exclusion of women. Women did not win the vote in France during the Revolution, and most Enlightenment thinkers did not support women's equality.

**Question 8: (B) — Abolition of the Slave Trade**  
Answer: Most slave systems depended on a constant supply of newly captured Africans.  
Why: Without new captives, most slave populations shrank, so the system declined; the U.S. was an exception because its enslaved population grew naturally. Agriculture was not fully mechanized, and only Haiti won freedom through revolution.

**Question 9: (A) — Robert Owen and Utopian Socialism**  
Answer: demonstrate a cooperative alternative to the problems of industrial capitalism.  
Why: Utopian socialists created model communities to show how society could be improved peacefully. Marx, not Owen, called for working-class revolution, and Owen criticized rather than defended unregulated markets.

**Question 10: (B) — Zionism and the Dreyfus Affair**  
Answer: a nationalist response to anti-Semitism seeking a Jewish homeland.  
Why: Zionism applied the idea that a people sharing a culture should have its own state, driven by anti-Semitism and pogroms. Ottomanism was a different, imperial policy, and Zionism was explicitly political.

## Topic 5.2: Nationalism and Revolutions

**Question 11: (A) — Causes of the American Revolution**  
Answer: colonists' experience of self-rule and opposition to mercantilism.  
Why: The passage stresses colonial self-rule and free-market objections to mercantilism. Slavery was not abolished in the colonies, there was no peasant revolt against nobles, and Napoleon's wars came later.

**Question 12: (D) — The Estates-General of 1789**  
Answer: commoners' demand for political representation proportional to their numbers.  
Why: Commoners left because the voting system let a small minority outvote them. The king actually threatened to arrest the leaders, and the nobles later refused to accept even a limited monarchy.

**Question 13: (C) — Connections Among Atlantic Revolutions**  
Answer: Revolutions in the Atlantic world were connected by both ideas and economic pressures.  
Why: French aid to the Americans deepened the debt that forced the king to call the Estates-General, linking the two revolutions. The passage directly contradicts the idea that France was financially strong.

**Question 14: (B) — Toussaint L'Ouverture's Constitution**  
Answer: overturned a slave-based social order and redistributed plantation land.  
Why: Both revolutions drew on Enlightenment ideas and fought a European power, so those options do not show a difference. Only Haiti abolished slavery and broke up plantations, transforming the social order.

**Question 15: (D) — Creole Fear of the Masses**  
Answer: Independence movements kept existing social hierarchies largely in place.  
Why: Because creoles excluded the masses, they remained a conservative upper class after independence; Peru, for example, denied most indigenous people the vote until 1860. That is the opposite of radical social change.

**Question 16: (D) — Creole Grievances**  
Answer: economic and political frustrations of a colonial elite.  
Why: Creoles objected to mercantilism (economic) and exclusion from office (political). They were an elite, not enslaved workers, and the passage does not mention religion.

**Question 17: (C) — Caudillos in Latin America**  
Answer: did not produce stable governments based on the rule of law.  
Why: Caudillos generally ignored representative government and the rule of law. Bolívar's hope for a U.S.-style federation (Gran Colombia) did not last, and military leaders became more, not less, involved in politics.

**Question 18: (B) — José Rizal and the Propaganda Movement**  
Answer: Repression of moderate reformers can intensify nationalist movements.  
Why: Spain's execution of a reformer who sought only autonomy pushed Filipinos toward revolution. The movement was led by educated elites influenced by European republicanism, and Spain did not grant independence.

**Question 19: (C) — Bismarck and German Unification**  
Answer: realpolitik using war and diplomacy for national goals.  
Why: Bismarck practiced realpolitik, politics based on power rather than ideals. Plebiscites were a feature of Italian unification, and Ottomanism was an Ottoman policy.

**Question 20: (A) — Ottomanism**  
Answer: It sharpened subject peoples' sense of difference and desire for independence.  
Why: Ironically, pressure to assimilate made ethnic and religious groups more suspicious and more nationalist. Balkan nationalism continued, and Russia and Austria kept supporting independence movements.

## Topic 5.3: Industrial Revolution Begins

**Question 21: (A) — Adam Smith's Pin Factory**  
Answer: the division of labor that characterized factory production.  
Why: Splitting one product into many specialized tasks is the division of labor. The cottage industry and guild artisans made whole products, and enclosure concerned land, not manufacturing.

**Question 22: (C) — The Agricultural Revolution**  
Answer: supplying more factory workers and more buyers for goods.  
Why: A larger, better-fed population supplied factory labor and markets, and efficient farming freed people from agriculture. Britain still imported raw cotton, and fewer, not more, people were needed on farms.

**Question 23: (D) — Indian Cotton and British Industry**  
Answer: It depended on global trade and coerced labor abroad.  
Why: British textiles grew out of competition with Indian cloth and relied on slave-grown American cotton. Steel and chemicals belong to the second industrial revolution, and British industry was mainly private.

**Question 24: (C) — The Cottage Industry**  
Answer: Bulky water-powered spinning machines that had to be housed in factories.  
Why: Arkwright's water frame (1769) was too large for homes, so production moved to factories. Demand for cotton was rising, and the telegraph and telephone came much later.

**Question 25: (A) — Richard Arkwright**  
Answer: concentrated production, machines, and workers in large buildings.  
Why: The water frame required factories, giving rise to the factory system. Hargreaves's spinning jenny was a home machine, Whitney created interchangeable parts, and oil became important much later.

**Question 26: (B) — Interchangeable Parts**  
Answer: Less need for skilled artisans and more repetitive, specialized work.  
Why: Interchangeable parts led to the division of labor and later Ford's assembly line, replacing skilled craftsmen with workers doing one task. This lowered, rather than raised, the value of workers' skills.

**Question 27: (B) — Britain's Coal**  
Answer: powered steam engines and was needed to make iron.  
Why: Burning coal powered steam engines and separated iron from its ore, enabling bridges, buildings, and ships. Water frames ran on water, not coal, and Britain still relied on rivers and canals for transport.

**Question 28: (A) — Capital and the Slave Trade**  
Answer: profits from coerced labor helped finance the Industrial Revolution.  
Why: The text links slave-trade profits to the investment capital that private entrepreneurs used. Britain did not ban the slave trade until 1807, and industrial demand increased interest in colonies.

**Question 29: (D) — Protection of Private Property**  
Answer: An institutional factor that encouraged investment.  
Why: Legal protection of property is an institution created by law, not geography, population, or technology. AP questions often ask you to classify factors this way.

**Question 30: (B) — The Enclosure Movement**  
Answer: creating an urban workforce for new factories.  
Why: Landless farmers migrated to cities and became factory workers. Enclosure reduced small farms and moved people into, not out of, cities.

## Topic 5.4: Industrialization Spreads

**Question 31: (B) — Delayed Industrialization in France**  
Answer: industrialized later than Britain.  
Why: Limited urban labor and the cost of revolution and war delayed French industrialization, but France did industrialize after Belgium. The passage says nothing about a Japanese-style state program.

**Question 32: (D) — German Unification and Industry**  
Answer: Political unification helped speed industrial growth.  
Why: The same resources produced little until unification removed political barriers. Germany industrialized after Britain, and unification strengthened its heavy industry.

**Question 33: (A) — Human Capital in the United States**  
Answer: a large workforce from immigration and internal migration.  
Why: The textbook calls this human capital. The U.S. was rich in resources, and its factories were mostly privately owned.

**Question 34: (C) — Russian Industrialization**  
Answer: significant in railroads and heavy industry but limited across the wider economy.  
Why: Russia built railroads and steel output, yet most people still farmed, so the change was uneven. Britain, Germany, and the U.S. led industry, and Russian growth centered on railroads and heavy industry rather than textiles.

**Question 35: (D) — Defensive Modernization in Japan**  
Answer: defensive modernization.  
Why: Japan adopted Western methods to defend itself and its culture, which the textbook calls defensive modernization. Deindustrialization describes India and Egypt, not Japan.

**Question 36: (C) — Indian Shipbuilding**  
Answer: colonial rule weakening an existing Asian industry.  
Why: British control replaced Indian shipbuilding and naval power with British dominance. This contributed to the decline of Asia's share of world manufacturing.

**Question 37: (A) — The Arms Act of 1878**  
Answer: fear of another rebellion.  
Why: The British feared Indians would arm themselves for another uprising, so they restricted mining and firearms. These policies destroyed, rather than developed, Indian mining.

**Question 38: (D) — Bombay Textile Mills**  
Answer: colonial governments used economic policy to protect industries in the home country.  
Why: The tax cut Bombay mills' profits to help Lancashire mills. It contradicted free trade and discouraged, rather than encouraged, Indian competition.

**Question 39: (B) — Egyptian Textiles**  
Answer: competition from mass-produced European factory goods.  
Why: Cheaper European machine-made cloth captured both foreign and Egyptian markets. European demand for textiles grew, and Egypt did not refuse trade or ban imports.

**Question 40: (C) — Global Shifts in Manufacturing**  
Answer: Industrial states' productivity and colonial policies undercut older producers.  
Why: Higher productivity in industrial Europe, plus colonial policies such as India's Arms Act and Bombay tax, shrank Asia's and the Middle East's share. These regions still produced goods, so the decline was relative, not total.

## Topic 5.5: Technology in the Industrial Age

**Question 41: (B) — Emerson on Railroads**  
Answer: opened access to natural resources and increased the distribution of goods.  
Why: Emerson saw railroads as unlocking the land's resources, matching the textbook's point that engines increased access to resources and distribution of goods. He initially viewed technology positively, not as an economic burden.

**Question 42: (D) — Coal-Powered Steam**  
Answer: mobile and dependable.  
Why: Steam power could be used anywhere, on demand, freeing factories, trains, and ships from rivers and wind. Coal was neither free nor renewable, and it polluted heavily.

**Question 43: (B) — Coaling Stations**  
Answer: increased imperial interest in controlling strategic ports and islands.  
Why: Steamships needed regular refueling, so powers sought control of ports along major routes. Steam power expanded trade and imperialism.

**Question 44: (A) — Coke and Wrought Iron**  
Answer: increased the output and usefulness of iron for transportation and industry.  
Why: Coke and wrought iron let producers make more, and more workable, iron. Both depended on coal and came before mass steel production, which followed in 1856.

**Question 45: (C) — The Bessemer Process**  
Answer: The second industrial revolution.  
Why: Steel, chemicals, precision machinery, and electronics define the second industrial revolution of the late 1800s. The other options came earlier.

**Question 46: (C) — Oil and the Internal Combustion Engine**  
Answer: New technologies changed how an energy source was used and valued over time.  
Why: Oil's main product shifted from kerosene to gasoline as engines developed. The first industrial revolution ran on coal, and cars appeared in the early 1900s.

**Question 47: (B) — Electrification**  
Answer: Changes in urban life through new forms of lighting and transportation.  
Why: Street lights and electric streetcars transformed industrial cities. Electricity spread first to cities in industrial countries and did not end coal use.

**Question 48: (A) — The Telephone and Radio**  
Answer: faster, worldwide communication.  
Why: The telephone and radio built on the telegraph to make near-instant global communication possible. This increased, rather than reduced, global connection.

**Question 49: (D) — The Transcontinental Railroad**  
Answer: Government funding was important in building transport infrastructure.  
Why: Public subsidies show that even a market economy used government support for infrastructure. Railroads opened, rather than closed, the interior.

**Question 50: (A) — Industrial Technology and Imperialism**  
Answer: imperial expansion in the late 1800s.  
Why: Industrial demand for raw materials and markets pushed states to seize colonies. The passage links technology to expanding, not shrinking, global networks.

## Topic 5.6: Industrialization: Government's Role

**Question 51: (B) — "The Sick Man of Europe"**  
Answer: corruption and nationalism, plus a failure to adopt Western technology.  
Why: The passage names corruption, nationalist unrest, and the failure to adopt Western technology and ideas. The empire did not industrialize rapidly and was not conquered by Japan.

**Question 52: (A) — Muhammad Ali's Reforms**  
Answer: state-sponsored modernization intended to strengthen Egypt.  
Why: The state directly built factories, schools, and a modern army, which is state-sponsored industrialization. Ali borrowed from Europe rather than rejecting it, and he took power from the Mamluks.

**Question 53: (B) — Financing Egyptian Industry**  
Answer: generate state revenue to fund military and industrial projects.  
Why: State control of land and cotton exports produced the money for factories and the army. Land moved from peasants to the state, and Egypt expanded its exports.

**Question 54: (C) — Commodore Perry in Japan**  
Answer: the end of Japan's isolation and pressure for rapid reform.  
Why: Forced opening revealed Japan's weakness and set the stage for the Meiji Restoration (1868). Japan gave in to the warships rather than defeating them.

**Question 55: (D) — Lessons from China**  
Answer: fear that Japan would suffer domination by Western powers.  
Why: Seeing China's defeat, reformers modernized to avoid the same fate while keeping their culture. They wanted to protect, not abandon, tradition.

**Question 56: (A) — Emperor Meiji's Letter**  
Answer: Selective adoption of Western institutions to gain equality.  
Why: Meiji described choosing institutions suited to Japan and adopting them gradually. This is selective adaptation, not total rejection or total copying.

**Question 57: (D) — Meiji Reforms**  
Answer: more directly led and funded by the central government.  
Why: The Meiji state planned and subsidized industry and changed institutions from the top, while British industrialization was led by private investors over about a century. Japan industrialized much faster than Britain.

**Question 58: (C) — Costs of Meiji Industrialization**  
Answer: Rapid state-led growth brought social costs like those in the West.  
Why: The textbook notes that Japan copied the exploitation of female mill workers seen in Britain. Growth was funded by a domestic land tax, not colonies.

**Question 59: (D) — The Zaibatsu**  
Answer: combined government initiative with growing private investment.  
Why: The state started industries, then sold many to private zaibatsu, and private investors drove innovation. Ownership shifted away from the state, and the zaibatsu were Japanese.

**Question 60: (C) — China's Limited Response**  
Answer: Japan's leaders embraced selective Westernization while China's weakened government resisted it.  
Why: Japan's leaders chose to adopt Western methods; China's weakened government resisted European influence and could not drive industrialization. Japan, not China, industrialized faster.

## Topic 5.7: Economic Developments and Innovations

**Question 61: (D) — Adam Smith on Human Nature**  
Answer: laissez-faire capitalism and private entrepreneurship.  
Why: If humans naturally trade, markets should be left free, which supported laissez-faire capitalism. Mercantilism, Marxism, and state ownership all limit free exchange.

**Question 62: (A) — The Corporation**  
Answer: lowering investors' risk and making investment more attractive.  
Why: Limited liability spread risk among many stockholders, so more people invested. It did not guarantee profits, and banks grew alongside corporations.

**Question 63: (C) — Monopolies**  
Answer: came to dominate their industries by eliminating competition.  
Why: A monopoly is control of a business with competition eliminated. These were private firms in industry, not agriculture.

**Question 64: (A) — Cecil Rhodes and African Railroads**  
Answer: They extracted resources while keeping colonial labor costs low.  
Why: The textbook describes railroads as a way to extract as many resources as possible while paying colonial workers as little as possible. The Cape-to-Cairo line was never completed.

**Question 65: (D) — HSBC**  
Answer: a transnational institution that supported global trade.  
Why: HSBC operated across national borders to finance trade and investment, linking Britain and Asia. It was British-owned and global in scope.

**Question 66: (B) — Unilever and Palm Oil**  
Answer: relied on colonial raw materials to supply factories in many countries.  
Why: Unilever used colonial palm oil for factories on several continents, gaining wealth and influence rarely seen before. It operated in, not away from, colonial territories.

**Question 67: (C) — Lloyd's of London**  
Answer: reducing the financial risk of overseas trade.  
Why: Insurance spread the risk of losing ships and cargo, encouraging more trade. Like corporations, it was a way to reduce business risk.

**Question 68: (B) — Advertising and Disposable Income**  
Answer: a culture of consumerism.  
Why: Mass production required mass buying, so advertising created a consumer culture aimed at the middle class. The other options are earlier or unrelated systems.

**Question 69: (B) — Sports and Social Class**  
Answer: Leisure reflected both business interests and class divisions.  
Why: Employers promoted sports to build disciplined workers, and specific sports followed class lines. Leisure grew rather than declined.

**Question 70: (A) — Music Halls and Public Parks**  
Answer: Using shared public spaces to shape working-class behavior.  
Why: Builders hoped mixing would lead workers to copy middle-class manners. The aim was influence, not legal equality, separation, or revolution.

## Topic 5.8: Reactions to the Industrial Economy

**Question 71: (C) — Mother Jones**  
Answer: draw attention to the harsh working conditions of coal miners.  
Why: Jones, a labor organizer, described miners who worked underground all day and never saw sunlight. Her goal was reform, not defense of owners or laissez-faire.

**Question 72: (D) — The Sadler Report**  
Answer: legislative efforts to improve working conditions.  
Why: By publicizing abuses, the report built support in Parliament for reform laws. It did not end factories or lead to a communist party.

**Question 73: (A) — Labor Unions in Britain**  
Answer: They faced early government hostility but eventually won significant reforms.  
Why: Unions began in secret and later gained legal acceptance and major benefits. They worked within capitalism rather than seeking to abolish property.

**Question 74: (D) — British Reform Bills**  
Answer: the gradual expansion of political participation in an industrial society.  
Why: Voting rights expanded step by step over nearly a century, through legislation rather than revolution. Cities gained, not lost, representation.

**Question 75: (D) — John Stuart Mill**  
Answer: gradual reform within the capitalist system.  
Why: Mill saw himself as a moderate advocate of gradual reform. Marx called for revolution, and Mill criticized unregulated laissez-faire capitalism as inhumane.

**Question 76: (C) — The Communist Manifesto**  
Answer: called for replacing capitalism rather than reforming it.  
Why: Marx predicted socialism, then communism, would replace capitalism, while Mill wanted reform. Marx scorned utopian communities and saw owners and workers as opposed classes.

**Question 77: (C) — Mahmud II and the Janissaries**  
Answer: centralize control of the army and revenue.  
Why: By eliminating the Janissaries and paying soldiers from the central treasury, Mahmud tied the army to the state. He used European trainers and took tax collection away from officers.

**Question 78: (B) — The Hatt-i Humayun**  
Answer: that reform could provoke opposition from groups with very different concerns.  
Why: Both Christians and Muslims opposed the edict for opposite reasons. The textbook notes the reforms did not achieve religious equality.

**Question 79: (A) — The Hundred Days of Reform**  
Answer: conservative resistance limited reform efforts in Qing China.  
Why: Cixi reversed the reforms, showing how conservative elites blocked change. The textbook says Europeans encouraged change in China, and the Qing lasted until 1911.

**Question 80: (B) — Samurai Resistance in Japan**  
Answer: a backlash by an elite that lost status through modernization.  
Why: The samurai lost their class, swords, and privileges, making them the main victims of Meiji modernization. Their resistance was conservative, not a labor movement.

## Topic 5.9: Society and the Industrial Age

**Question 81: (A) — Dickens's Oliver Twist**  
Answer: the gap between the prosperous and the urban poor.  
Why: Dickens contrasts the well-housed with the homeless, starving poor of industrial cities. He was criticizing inequality, not praising industrialization.

**Question 82: (B) — Tenements and Public Health**  
Answer: Governments responded to urban problems by expanding public health regulation.  
Why: Cities passed public health acts and created police and fire departments in response to slum conditions. Cities kept growing as people moved there.

**Question 83: (B) — The New Working Class**  
Answer: industrial wages stayed low for many workers.  
Why: Low-skilled, replaceable workers competing for jobs could not demand higher pay. Skills became less valuable, and labor was plentiful.

**Question 84: (D) — Captains of Industry**  
Answer: land ownership to industrial wealth.  
Why: Wealth from industry and investment replaced inherited land as the main source of power. This is the opposite of a return to landed status.

**Question 85: (C) — The Factory Whistle**  
Answer: Work was set by the clock rather than by the worker or the seasons.  
Why: Factory time discipline replaced the flexible rhythms of farm work. Workers lost control of their time, worked long hours, and left their families during the workday.

**Question 86: (B) — Child Labor**  
Answer: that adult wages were too low to support the family.  
Why: Families needed every possible income because factory wages were low. Later laws actually limited child labor and required schooling.

**Question 87: (A) — Women in Factories**  
Answer: Industrialization created jobs for women while reinforcing wage inequality.  
Why: Women gained factory jobs, but owners hired them because they could pay them less. This expanded women's work without equal pay.

**Question 88: (A) — The Cult of Domesticity**  
Answer: middle-class ideals of separate roles for men and women.  
Why: The ideal reflected middle-class families that could live on one income, with the husband working away from home. For working-class women it added housework to full-time jobs.

**Question 89: (C) — Smog and Pollution**  
Answer: Environmental damage that harmed human health.  
Why: Burning fossil fuels and dumping waste created smog and polluted water, spreading disease. Coal remained the main fuel.

**Question 90: (D) — Global Inequality**  
Answer: a widening gap between industrial states and other regions.  
Why: Early industrializers exploited other regions for resources and launched new colonization, widening the global gap. Demand for raw materials rose.

## Topic 5.10: Continuity and Change in the Industrial Age

**Question 91: (C) — The Communist Manifesto on Capital**  
Answer: criticize private ownership of the means of production.  
Why: Marx and Engels argued that capital, produced collectively, should not be privately controlled. Adam Smith, not Marx, defended private profit.

**Question 92: (C) — Scale of Change**  
Answer: the exceptional scale of the changes caused by industrialization.  
Why: Comparing it to the Agricultural Revolution shows how transformative industrialization was. Agriculture did not disappear; most people worldwide still farmed.

**Question 93: (D) — Sources of Raw Materials**  
Answer: Many regions kept supplying raw materials to Europe.  
Why: These regions kept producing resources for export, as they had before, even as new technology expanded the scale. Europe still depended on imports.

**Question 94: (A) — From Artisans to Assembly Lines**  
Answer: Consumer goods became more available, more affordable, and more varied.  
Why: Mass production made goods cheaper and more plentiful, even as it reduced the role of skilled artisans.

**Question 95: (B) — The Changing Middle Class**  
Answer: both continuity and change in the middle class.  
Why: Doctors, lawyers, and shopkeepers stayed (continuity) while managerial jobs were added (change). The middle class grew rather than disappeared.

**Question 96: (D) — Women's Work and Wages**  
Answer: Women's economic role changed, but wage inequality continued.  
Why: Women's paid work became essential to families (change), yet unequal pay persisted (continuity). This is a classic continuity-and-change pattern.

**Question 97: (D) — The Revolutions of 1848**  
Answer: Liberalism and nationalism.  
Why: Press freedom and parliaments are liberal demands; freedom from Austria is nationalist. The 1848 revolutions combined both.

**Question 98: (A) — The Middle Class and Democracy**  
Answer: the size and influence of the middle class.  
Why: The textbook links democracy to a large middle class. Where that class was small, older authoritarian systems continued.

**Question 99: (C) — Bismarck's Social Reforms**  
Answer: reduce the appeal of socialism by addressing workers' needs.  
Why: Bismarck used limited reforms to prevent more radical change. His reforms later spread throughout Europe and the world.

**Question 100: (B) — Labor Parties**  
Answer: the extension of voting rights to working-class men.  
Why: Once workers could vote, they formed parties to win reforms through elections. Labor parties grew alongside unions and industry, not after their decline.
