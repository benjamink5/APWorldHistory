## Big Picture

- Charles Dickens (Oliver Twist, 1839) contrasted the prosperous new middle class with the mistreated urban poor.
- Unplanned urban growth created slums, disease, and pollution, but over time reforms and industry raised living standards for many.
- A new class hierarchy formed: captains of industry at the top, a white-collar middle class, and a low-skilled working class.
- Work left the home, children labored in mills and mines, and women's lives changed differently by class.
- Industrialization increased global inequality and ushered in a second wave of colonization.

## 1. Effects on Urban Areas

- In the first half of the 1800s cities grew rapidly with little government planning, leaving a damaging ecological footprint.
- Working families crowded into shoddy tenements, often owned by factory owners, in slums with polluted water and open sewers.
- Cholera spread quickly, along with fire, crime, and violence.
- Over time cities created police and fire departments, and public health acts improved drainage, sewage, clean water, rubbish removal, and building standards.
- Eventually living standards rose for many; middle-class wealth and opportunity kept drawing rural people to cities, and many did find a better life.

## 2. Effects on Class Structure

- Working class (bottom): factory and mine laborers; interchangeable parts and division of labor took away the craft of making a whole product, so managers saw workers as replaceable, and competition kept wages low.
- Middle class: factory and office managers, small business owners, and professionals — literate white-collar workers.
- Top: industrialists and owners of large corporations — "captains of industry" — who overshadowed the landed aristocracy.

## 3. Farm Work vs. Factory Work

- Before industrialization, families worked close together at home or in the fields; large factory machines made home work impossible, so people left family and neighborhood for long workdays.
- The factory whistle set breaks — a culture shock for former farmers who had set their own schedules.
- Workers commonly worked 14 hours a day, six days a week; exhausted workers on heavy machinery suffered frequent injuries and deaths.

## 4. Effects on Children

- Low wages forced families to send children to work; children as young as five worked in textile mills.
- Small size and nimble fingers let them repair machines and squeeze into tight spots, but textile dust damaged their lungs.
- Coal mines were worse: oppressive heat, heavy loads, coal dust, and constant threats of collapse and flooding.

## 5. Effects on Women's Lives

- Working-class women worked in coal mines (until Britain banned it in the 1840s) and were the main laborers in textile factories; owners preferred them because they could pay women half of men's wages.
- Middle-class women were spared factory work but lived more limited lives; a stay-at-home wife was a status symbol showing her husband could support the family.
- Late-1800s advertising and pamphlets built a "cult of domesticity" idealizing the homemaker as pious, submissive, pure, and domestic; for working-class women it meant housework and childcare on top of full-time jobs.
- Industrialization also spurred feminism: when men left for jobs, women at home gained new opportunities; 300 people met at Seneca Falls (1848) to call for women's equality.

## 6. Effects on the Environment

- Industry ran on fossil fuels — coal, petroleum, natural gas; coal gave more energy than wood but was extremely harmful.
- Smog (smoke plus fog) from coal-burning factories caused deadly respiratory problems in late-1800s industrial towns.
- Factories dumped waste into rivers and lakes; cholera, typhoid, and other diseases ravaged neighborhoods.

## 7. The Industrial Revolution's Legacy

- Mass production made goods cheaper, more abundant, and more accessible than ever.
- Factories drew people from farms to cities and from agrarian to industrial countries — both low-skilled workers and professionals.
- Work moved from homes to factories, changing family life; a new and many said unequal relationship formed between workers and owners; crowding and poverty brought more crime.
- Global inequality grew: early industrializers searched the world for raw materials such as cotton and rubber, undercut early industrialization in Egypt, China, and India, and launched a second wave of colonization.

## Comparison: The New Class Hierarchy

| Class | Who | Work and skills | Position |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper | Industrialists, owners of large corporations ("captains of industry") | Investment and ownership | Overshadowed the landed aristocracy |
| Middle | Factory and office managers, small business owners, professionals | Educated, literate, white-collar | Growing access to goods, housing, culture |
| Working | Factory and mine laborers | Low-skilled, one repetitive task | Seen as replaceable; low wages |

## Comparison: Working-Class vs. Middle-Class Women

| Feature | Working-class women | Middle-class women |
|---|---|---|
| Paid work | Coal mines (until 1840s), textile factories, domestic service | Spared factory work |
| Pay | About half of men's wages | No economic role |
| Cult of domesticity | Full-time job plus housework and childcare | Idealized homemaker; status symbol |
| Limits | Exhaustion and danger | Limited by household roles |

## Comparison: Farm Work vs. Factory Work

| Feature | Farm / cottage work | Factory work |
|---|---|---|
| Place | Home or nearby fields, with family | Factory, away from family |
| Schedule | Set by one's own needs | Set by the factory whistle |
| Hours | Varied by season | About 14 hours a day, 6 days a week |
| Skills | Crafting a whole product | One repetitive task |

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Tenement** | Crowded, shoddily built apartment building for working families |
| **Slum** | Area of a city where low-income families were forced to live |
| **Public health acts** | Laws improving sanitation, sewers, water, and building standards |
| **Working class** | Laborers in factories and coal mines |
| **Middle class** | Managers, small business owners, and professionals |
| **White-collar worker** | Office worker in a managerial or professional job |
| **Captains of industry** | Industrialists and big-corporation owners at the top of society |
| **Landed aristocracy** | Old elite whose wealth came from land |
| **Child labor** | Children working in mills and mines |
| **Cult of domesticity** | Ideal of women as pious, submissive, pure, domestic homemakers |
| **Feminism** | Movement for women's equality |
| **Smog** | Smoke and fog from coal-burning factories |
| **Second wave of colonization** | New colonial expansion driven by industrial demand for raw materials |
