## 1. Key Terms

- [ ] **What do dharma, karma, and moksha mean in Hinduism?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Dharma is the duty that fits a person's place in life and caste. Karma is the idea that actions shape the next life. Moksha is release from the cycle of rebirth (samsara).</details>

- [ ] **What are varna and jati in the caste system?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Varna is one of four broad classes: Brahmins (priests), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (laborers). Jati is a birth group, usually tied to an occupation, that people married within. "Untouchable" groups (Dalits) were outside the varnas.</details>

- [ ] **What are the Four Noble Truths?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Life is suffering; suffering comes from desire; suffering can end; and the Eightfold Path ends it. The goal is nirvana.</details>

- [ ] **What is Buddhist monasticism?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Life of monks and nuns in communities (the sangha) who gave up property, studied, and meditated. Kings gave monasteries land, and laypeople gave gifts to earn merit. Monasteries were centers of learning. The textbook says that in Sri Lanka, monasteries and nunneries flourished, both men and women chose a life of contemplation, and Buddhist priests often advised the monarchs.</details>

- [ ] **What is Theravada Buddhism, and where was it strong c. 1200–1450?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The branch of Buddhism that follows the oldest texts. It was strong in Sri Lanka and spread from there to Burma, Thailand, and Cambodia.</details>

- [ ] **What was the Bhakti movement?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A Hindu movement of intense personal devotion to a single god (often Vishnu, Krishna, or Shiva), stressing emotion instead of studying texts or performing rituals. It rejected caste hierarchy, was open to women and people of low status, and used vernacular poetry and song instead of Sanskrit. The textbook says it began in southern India in the 12th century; many accounts trace its roots to the Tamil Alvars and Nayanars of the 6th–9th centuries.</details>

- [ ] **What is Sufism?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The mystical branch of Islam. Sufis sought a personal experience of God through prayer, poetry, music, and dance. They were the most important missionaries of Islam in South and Southeast Asia.</details>

- [ ] **What was the jizya?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A tax paid by non-Muslims under Muslim rule. In the Delhi Sultanate, non-Muslims paid it and were treated as protected people. The textbook says the jizya on all non-Muslim subjects made many Hindus resent Muslims and see them as foreigners.</details>

- [ ] **What is syncretism? Give one example from South Asia.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The blending of beliefs from different traditions. Example: Kabir blended Hindu Bhakti and Muslim Sufi ideas, teaching that God is one, whether called Rama or Allah. Another: the Hindu poet Lal Ded of Kashmir was admired by many Sufis.</details>

- [ ] **What does it mean to proselytize, and why is Islam called a universalizing religion?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>To proselytize is to actively seek converts. A universalizing religion, such as Islam or Buddhism, seeks converts from all peoples. The textbook says Muslim rulers in India found that forcing Hindus and Buddhists to convert did not work, so most converts came to Islam voluntarily.</details>

- [ ] **What do the Qutb Minar, Urdu, and "Arabic numerals" show about cultural exchange in South Asia?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The Qutb Minar (textbook: Qutub Minar) is a huge tower in Delhi rising over a mosque that Delhi Sultanate rulers built on top of a Hindu temple; it symbolized Islamic influence. Urdu combined Hindi grammar with Arabic and some Persian (Farsi) vocabulary and is now Pakistan's official language. "Arabic numerals" actually came from India, and Indian algebra and geometry spread through Arabic translations. (The textbook calls the Qutub Minar India's tallest structure; many accounts call it the world's tallest brick minaret. Many accounts also say Urdu's borrowed words are mostly Persian.)</details>

## 2. People

- [ ] **Who was Kabir?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A 15th-century weaver poet from Varanasi (dates disputed). He blended Hindu and Muslim ideas, criticized empty ritual in both faiths, and wrote in Hindi. He is a key example of Bhakti and syncretism.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Mirabai?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A Rajput princess (c. 1498–1547) devoted to Krishna and a famous female Bhakti poet. The textbook (spelling "Mira Bai") names her one of the most famous Bhakti figures, living in the 16th century. She lived after 1450, so she shows Bhakti's continuing influence and its openness to women.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Moinuddin Chishti?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A Sufi saint of the Chishti order who settled at Ajmer (d. 1236). His shrine drew both Muslims and Hindus and helped spread Islam in India.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Timur, and what did he do in 1398?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A Turkic-Mongol conqueror from Central Asia (also called Tamerlane). He sacked Delhi in 1398, and the Delhi Sultanate never fully recovered.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Jayavarman VII?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A Khmer king (r. 1181–c. 1218) and Mahayana Buddhist. He built Angkor Thom and the Bayon temple, plus roads, rest houses, and hospitals, moving the Khmer Empire toward Buddhism.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Gajah Mada?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The chief minister of Majapahit in the 14th century. He vowed to unite the archipelago and helped Majapahit reach its peak with King Hayam Wuruk. The textbook says Majapahit had 98 tributaries at its height.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Lal Ded?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Lal Ded, or Mother Lalla (1320–1392), was a Hindu poet from Kashmir in northern India. Her stress on personal experience appealed to many Muslims, especially Sufis. The textbook opens Topic 1.3 with her poem to show the interaction between Hindu and Muslim traditions.</details>

## 3. Dates & Events

- [ ] **550**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The Gupta Dynasty collapses, ending India's Classical "Golden Age." Disunity returns for most of the next 1,000 years: north and south India develop separate political structures, and competing Rajput clan kingdoms gradually form in the north.</details>

- [ ] **1206**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Qutb al-Din Aibak founds the Delhi Sultanate in north India. The textbook says Islamic forces conquered Delhi in the early 13th century and the sultanate ruled for about 300 years.</details>

- [ ] **1293**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Raden Wijaya founds Majapahit on Java after using and then expelling a Mongol (Yuan) army. The textbook dates Majapahit to 1293–1520; other accounts end it c. 1478 or c. 1527.</details>

- [ ] **1336**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The brothers Harihara and Bukka found the Hindu Vijayanagara Empire ("the victorious city") in south India. The textbook says the Delhi Sultanate had sent them south, and that they were born Hindu, converted to Islam for upward mobility, and returned to Hinduism once free of Delhi; many historians treat this as a debated traditional story.</details>

- [ ] **c. 1400**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Malacca is founded on the Strait of Malacca; its ruler soon converts to Islam (see 2.3).</details>

- [ ] **c. 1431**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Thai forces take Angkor, and the Khmer capital moves south (traditional date). The textbook says the Thais of the Sukhothai Kingdom forced the Khmers out; many accounts say it was the Thai kingdom of Ayutthaya, which had made Sukhothai its vassal.</details>

- [ ] **1526**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Babur defeats the last Lodi sultan at Panipat; the Delhi Sultanate ends and the Mughal Empire begins. The textbook notes that the Mughals traced their ancestry to the Mongols.</details>

## 4. Cause & Effect

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: Why did Srivijaya grow rich and powerful?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>It controlled the Strait of Malacca, where ships between India and China waited for the monsoon winds. The textbook says it built up its navy and charged fees on ships traveling between India and China. It also fought pirates and sent tribute to China, so trade wealth flowed to its ports.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did Sufis spread Islam in South and Southeast Asia?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Sufis lived among ordinary people, built shrines open to all, and used local languages and music. Their message of love of God and spiritual equality appealed to many. The textbook adds that Sufis in Southeast Asia were tolerant of local faiths, so converts could be Muslims and still honor local deities. Islam spread through gradual, mostly peaceful conversion.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did the Bhakti movement affect Indian society?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>By teaching that anyone could reach god through devotion, it challenged caste hierarchy and opened religion to women and lower castes. It produced vernacular literature and helped Hinduism stay strong under Muslim rule. The textbook says that just as the Sufis helped spread Islam, the Bhaktis helped spread Hinduism.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did the Delhi Sultanate rule a Hindu majority, and what limited its power?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>As a Muslim minority, it relied on cavalry armies and taxes, including the jizya on non-Muslims, and let many Hindu chiefs keep local power, which helped it last over 300 years. The textbook stresses its limits: it never built an efficient Chinese-style bureaucracy, so local kingdoms kept major roles; the jizya made many Hindus resent Muslims; and defending against the Mongols halted its push south.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did irrigation support Khmer power?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Huge reservoirs (barays) and canals stored water for rice. The textbook says irrigation allowed several rice harvests a year and drainage reduced monsoon damage, making the Khmer one of Southeast Asia's most prosperous kingdoms. Rice surpluses fed a large population and paid taxes, and kings used that labor and wealth to build temples at Angkor that showed their legitimacy. Sri Lanka's Sinhala kings used similar reservoirs, called tanks.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: Why did Buddhism decline in India but survive in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>In India, Hindu devotion absorbed many Buddhist ideas, and raids destroyed monasteries such as Nalanda. The textbook adds that corruption among monks left Buddhism disorganized and says Buddhists were the largest group of converts to Islam (many accounts say Buddhism was already shrinking before the conquests). In Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, kings protected and funded monasteries, and monks spread Theravada Buddhism.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: Why did the Khmer Empire decline in the 15th century?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Wars with the Thais, strain on the water system, and the shift to Theravada Buddhism (which needed fewer royal temples) weakened royal power. In 1431 Thai forces took Angkor, and the capital moved south. The textbook names the Sukhothai Kingdom; many accounts say Ayutthaya.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: Why did South Asians convert to Islam, and how much did conversion change society?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The textbook says most converts came voluntarily: Muslim merchants settled in port cities and their wives often converted, and low-caste Hindus were drawn by Islam's equality of believers. But change was limited. Low-caste converts usually did not raise their status, the flexible caste system (India's strongest continuity) absorbed Muslims as occupation-based subcastes, and Islam did not greatly change gender relations.</details>

## 5. Comparison & AP Writing

- [ ] **Compare: How were Srivijaya and Majapahit similar and different?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Similar: both were maritime states that used navies to control sea routes and Indian Ocean trade. Different: Srivijaya (Sumatra) was mainly Buddhist and depended almost entirely on charging ships in the straits. Majapahit (Java) was Hindu-Buddhist, had 98 tributaries at its height, and combined sea power with rice farming. The 1.3 textbook calls Srivijaya "primarily a Buddhist kingdom" and Majapahit "a Hindu kingdom (but with strong Buddhist influences)"; the 2.5 reading says the reverse.</details>

- [ ] **Compare: How did land-based and sea-based states maintain power?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Land-based states (Khmer, Vijayanagara, Rajputs, Sinhala) relied on agriculture, irrigation, armies, forts, and temples. Sea-based states (Srivijaya, Majapahit) relied on navies, control of straits and ports, alliances with local rulers, and trade taxes.</details>

- [ ] **Compare: How were the Bhakti movement and Sufism similar and different?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Similar: the textbook calls both mystical movements that stressed inner reflection and a direct personal relationship with the divine; both placed love of God over ritual, used poetry and song, appealed to people outside their traditions, and led to syncretism (Kabir, later Sikhism). Different: Bhakti came from Hinduism and focused on one Hindu god; Sufism came from Islam and spread Islam through conversion.</details>

- [ ] **Compare: How did Hinduism and Islam differ, according to the textbook?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Hindus pray to many gods, while Islam is strictly monotheistic. Hindu temples and art are full of images of deities, while Muslims disapprove of any visual representation of Allah. Hinduism was tied to a hierarchical caste system, while Islam calls for the equality of all believers. Hindus recognize several sacred texts, while Muslims look primarily to the Quran.</details>

- [ ] **AP Short Answer: Explain one way a belief system affected society in South Asia from c. 1200 to c. 1450.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** The Bhakti movement challenged the caste system in South Asian society.<br/>
**Evidence:** Bhakti poet-saints such as Kabir taught that anyone could reach god through personal devotion and wrote in vernacular languages instead of Sanskrit.<br/>
**Reasoning:** Because devotion did not require Brahmin priests or costly rituals, religion opened to women and lower castes, which weakened the religious basis of caste hierarchy even though caste continued.
</details>

- [ ] **AP Short Answer: Explain one way a Southeast Asian state maintained power from c. 1200 to c. 1450.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** Srivijaya maintained power by controlling sea trade through the Strait of Malacca.<br/>
**Evidence:** It built up its navy, charged fees on ships traveling between India and China, fought pirates, and sent tribute missions to China.<br/>
**Reasoning:** Control of this narrow passage between India and China gave Srivijaya steady trade wealth, which it used to keep local rulers loyal and to support Buddhist monasteries that made it a religious center of the region.
</details>

- [ ] **AP Long Essay Thesis: Evaluate the extent to which religion shaped state building in South and Southeast Asia from c. 1200 to c. 1450.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** Religion greatly shaped how states legitimized power, but economic foundations such as trade and irrigation decided how states maintained it.<br/>
**Evidence:** Khmer kings filled the temples of Angkor with Hindu and later Buddhist art, Sinhala kings protected Buddhist monasteries, and Vijayanagara presented itself as a Hindu state. However, Srivijaya's power came from taxing ships in the Strait of Malacca, and Khmer power rested on irrigated rice farming.<br/>
**Reasoning:** Religion gave rulers legitimacy and loyal subjects, but only steady wealth from trade or agriculture let them pay armies and build monuments, so religion and economy worked together.
</details>
