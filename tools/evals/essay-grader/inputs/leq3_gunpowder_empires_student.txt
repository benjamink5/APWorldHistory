Land-based empires in southern and southwestern Asia developed in similar ways to a large extent from 1450 to 1750.

In the 1300s and 1400s, the Mongol Empire and its successor states such as the Timurid Empire broke apart, leaving a power vacuum across Central and Southwest Asia. Turkic nomadic groups who had fought as cavalry for these states began to build their own states. At the same time, gunpowder, which had been invented in China, spread west along trade routes, and by the 1400s cannons and handguns were being used in the Islamic world. These developments allowed new rulers to conquer large territories and create the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires.

The first similarity is that all three empires used gunpowder weapons to expand. The Ottomans under Mehmed II used huge cannons to break the walls of Constantinople in 1453, ending the Byzantine Empire and making the city their capital. In 1526, Babur, the founder of the Mughal Empire, used field guns and matchlock muskets to defeat the much larger army of the Delhi Sultanate at the First Battle of Panipat. These victories show that gunpowder allowed both empires to conquer older states quickly.

The second similarity is that the rulers built armies that were loyal only to them. The Ottomans had the Janissaries, slave soldiers who were trained from childhood and armed with muskets. In Safavid Persia, Shah Abbas I created an army of ghulams, enslaved Christian soldiers from the Caucasus, to reduce his dependence on the Qizilbash tribal cavalry. In both cases, the ruler used a professional army to weaken the power of the old nobility.

The Mughal emperor Akbar, who built the Taj Mahal, also tried to unify his empire through religious tolerance.

In conclusion, the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires developed in similar ways to a large extent. However, the Safavids were Shi'a Muslims while the Ottomans were Sunni.
