Between 1450 and 1750, many empires existed in Eurasia. Rulers in Eurasia were largely successful in consolidating power because they built loyal bureaucracies and took control of weapons and land, although heavy taxes and harsh land systems sometimes caused resistance that limited their success.

First, rulers consolidated power by creating officials who depended on them. In Document 1, a village record from southern China shows that the early Ming government picked a tax captain in every township who collected all the grain and silver taxes, and village leaders were so afraid of him that they "did not dare sit" in front of him. This shows the Ming state reached all the way down to the villages through its own officials. Document 2 also shows this. Machiavelli explains that the Ottoman sultan divides his kingdom into sanjaks and sends administrators there, and he can move them whenever he wants because they are all his servants, so the empire is easy to hold once conquered. Machiavelli was Italian. The Ottomans also used the devshirme system, in which Christian boys from the Balkans were taken, converted to Islam, and trained as Janissaries or government officials. Because these men had no family power of their own and owed everything to the sultan, they were loyal to him, which made the sultan's control stronger.

Second, rulers took weapons away from groups that could challenge them. In Document 3, Hideyoshi, the Tokugawa shogun, ordered farmers to give up their swords, spears, and firearms, saying that weapons made it hard to collect rent and led to uprisings. By disarming the peasants, he made sure only the samurai class could fight, which helped bring peace to Japan after a long period of civil war.

However, consolidation was not always successful. In Document 5, François Bernier describes how the Mughal emperor granted land to soldiers and governors instead of paying them a salary, and these grantees had almost absolute power over the peasants. Bernier was a French traveler who was used to European ideas about private property, so he describes the system as cruel and tyrannical, and his point of view helps show that the emperor's control over the countryside depended on local officials who could abuse peasants and weaken the empire. Document 6 says "Louis XIV wished to reign by himself" and that "his love of admiration was his ruin."

In conclusion, rulers in Eurasia were largely successful in consolidating power through bureaucracies and control of weapons, but harsh systems sometimes created resistance.
