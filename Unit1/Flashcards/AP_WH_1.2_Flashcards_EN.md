## 1. Key Terms

- [ ] **What is Dar al-Islam, and why does the textbook still call it one region after it split politically?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>"The house of Islam": the lands under Muslim rule where sharia shaped law and society. By 1200 it stretched from al-Andalus to northern India, but it was no longer one state. The textbook stresses that it stayed one cultural region: trade spread goods and ideas, the common use of sharia created similar legal systems, and centers of learning in Baghdad, Córdoba, Cairo, and Bukhara (the textbook's "great universities") shared new ideas.</details>

- [ ] **What are the Five Pillars of Islam?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>(1) Shahada, the declaration of faith; (2) Salat, prayer five times a day; (3) Zakat, charity to the poor; (4) Sawm, fasting during Ramadan; (5) Hajj, pilgrimage to Mecca. They gave Muslims in very different regions the same daily practices, and the hajj brought Muslims from many lands together.</details>

- [ ] **What is sharia, and who were the ulama?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Sharia is Islamic law based on the Qur'an and the hadith (sayings and actions of Muhammad). Qadis (judges) applied it in courts, and it governed family life, trade, inheritance, and crime. The ulama were scholars of religion and law who taught in madrasas, advised rulers, and gave rulers religious legitimacy.</details>

- [ ] **Why did Sunni and Shia Muslims split?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>They disagreed over who should lead the community after Muhammad's death in 632. Sunnis (the majority) accepted caliphs chosen by the community. Shia believed leadership belonged to Ali and his descendants. Example: the Abbasids were Sunni; the Fatimids of Egypt were Shia.</details>

- [ ] **What is Sufism, and how did it differ from most Muslims' approach to faith?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The mystical tradition of Islam. Many Muslims focused on intellectual study of the Qur'an; Sufis stressed introspection (looking inward) to grasp truths they believed learning could not reach, using meditation, poetry, music, and dance. Sufism may have begun as a reaction to the perceived love of luxury of the early Umayyad Caliphate. Sufi missionaries sometimes blended local religious elements into Islam, which won many converts.</details>

- [ ] **What were dhimmi, and what was the jizya?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Dhimmi ("protected people") were non-Muslims, mainly Jews and Christians ("People of the Book"), under Muslim rule. They could practice their religion and keep their own leaders and courts, but they paid a special tax, the jizya, and faced some limits.</details>

- [ ] **How did the roles of the sultan and the caliph differ after 1055?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The sultan held political and military power. The caliph kept religious authority but was mainly a figurehead; the textbook says the highest-ranking Abbasid was reduced to the chief Sunni religious authority. The textbook says the Seljuk leader Tughril Beg "called himself sultan"; many accounts say the Abbasid caliph granted him the title.</details>

- [ ] **What was a mamluk, and why could mamluks rise higher than most enslaved people?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A slave-soldier, usually a Turk from Central Asia. Islam banned enslaving Muslims and other monotheists (Jews, Christians, Zoroastrians), so enslaved people were imported from Africa, Kievan Rus, and Central Asia. Boys were bought, converted, and trained as elite cavalry. Mamluks served as soldiers and later as bureaucrats, which gave them more chances for advancement. In 1250, mamluk officers seized power in Egypt and founded the Mamluk Sultanate.</details>

- [ ] **What was the House of Wisdom?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A renowned library and center of scholarship in Abbasid Baghdad, at its height under al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833). It drew scholars from far away, who translated Greek, Persian, and Indian works into Arabic. It was destroyed in the Mongol sack of 1258.</details>

- [ ] **What were hijab and the harem?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Hijab can mean dressing modestly or a specific head covering; most Muslim women observed it. Covering the head and face was an older custom in Central Asia and the Byzantine Empire that became more established under Islam. The harem was the part of a household set aside for wives, concubines, and their children; it symbolized women's new status in growing cities.</details>

## 2. People

- [ ] **Who was al-Khwarizmi?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A mathematician in Abbasid Baghdad (c. 780–c. 850). His book gave us the word algebra (al-jabr), his name gave us "algorithm," and he spread Hindu-Arabic numerals, including zero.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Ibn Sina (Avicenna)?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A Persian physician and philosopher (980–1037). His Canon of Medicine was used as a textbook in European universities for centuries.</details>

- [ ] **Who were Omar Khayyam and Rumi?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Two Persian writers under the Seljuks. Omar Khayyam (1048–1131) was a mathematician, astronomer, and poet who solved cubic equations geometrically, helped create a more accurate calendar, and is known for the Rubaiyat. Rumi (1207–1273), a Sufi poet in Seljuk Konya, wrote the Masnavi about the soul's love for God; his followers founded the Mevlevi order (the "whirling dervishes").</details>

- [ ] **Who was Ibn Rushd (Averroes)?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A qadi and philosopher from Córdoba (1126–1198) who worked in philosophy, law, and medicine and wrote detailed commentaries on Aristotle. Latin Europe called him "the Commentator." His work influenced Maimonides, who in turn influenced Thomas Aquinas and scholasticism.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Maimonides?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A Jewish philosopher (1138–1204; the textbook gives c. 1135–c. 1204) born in Córdoba who worked as a physician in Cairo. Influenced by Ibn Rushd, he combined Aristotle's reasoning with biblical interpretation in the Guide for the Perplexed, written in Arabic, and influenced Thomas Aquinas. He shows that Judaism continued to thrive inside Dar al-Islam.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Nasir al-Din al-Tusi?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A Persian scholar (1201–1274) who contributed to astronomy, law, logic, ethics, mathematics, philosophy, and medicine. Under the Mongol ruler Hulagu he directed the Maragha observatory (founded 1259), which the textbook calls the most advanced in the world. He created the "Tusi couple" model of planetary motion, and his work on the sides and angles of triangles laid the groundwork for trigonometry as a separate subject. The textbook places him in Baghdad's "golden age"; many accounts note he worked mainly at Maragha after 1258.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Hulagu?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A grandson of Chinggis Khan who sacked Baghdad in 1258 and ended the Abbasid Caliphate. He founded the Ilkhanate and also supported al-Tusi's Maragha observatory.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Ibn Khaldun?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A North African historian (1332–1406). His Muqaddimah explained why dynasties rise and fall, and he is widely seen as a founder of historiography (the study of historians' methods) and sociology.</details>

- [ ] **Who was 'A'ishah al-Ba'uniyyah?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A Sufi poet and mystic from Damascus, possibly the most prolific female Muslim writer before the 20th century. Her long poem Clear Inspiration, on Praise of the Trusted One honors Muhammad and shows her broad learning. The textbook gives her dates as 1460–1507; many reference works give her death as 1517.</details>

## 3. Dates & Events

- [ ] **732**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>At the Battle of Tours, Frankish forces defeat a Muslim army. Muslim forces had invaded Spain in 711 and made Córdoba their capital; Tours marked the limit of rapid Islamic expansion into Western Europe, though Muslims ruled parts of Spain for about seven more centuries.</details>

- [ ] **1055**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The Seljuk Turks under Tughril Beg enter Baghdad, and political power passes to the sultan while the caliph keeps religious authority. The textbook says Tughril called himself sultan; many accounts say the caliph granted the title.</details>

- [ ] **1085**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Christian Castile captures Toledo. Toledo then becomes a center for translating Arabic works into Latin.</details>

- [ ] **1206**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Qutb al-Din Aibak, a Turkic former slave general, founds the Delhi Sultanate in northern India.</details>

- [ ] **1250**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Mamluk officers seize power in Egypt and found the Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1517).</details>

- [ ] **1258**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The Mongols under Hulagu sack Baghdad, kill the last Abbasid caliph, and destroy libraries including the House of Wisdom. The textbook says this also ended Seljuk rule; many accounts say the Great Seljuk Empire had already ended in 1194 and the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum survived as a Mongol vassal until 1308.</details>

- [ ] **1260**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The Mamluks under Qutuz and Baybars defeat the Mongols at the Battle of Ain Jalut. It kept the Mongols out of Egypt, protected Mecca and Medina, and gave the new Mamluk Sultanate prestige; in 1261 the Mamluks set up a "shadow" Abbasid caliph in Cairo to strengthen their legitimacy. The textbook says the Mongols "were stopped in Egypt"; the battle was fought in Palestine.</details>

## 4. Cause & Effect

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: Why did the Abbasid Caliphate fragment, and what followed?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Causes: the empire was too large to control, governors became independent, rival caliphates arose (Córdoba, the Fatimids), and caliphs relied on Turkic slave-soldiers. The textbook adds four outside challengers (Mamluks, Seljuks, Crusaders, Mongols) and economic competition: trade shifted to routes farther north, and Baghdad lost wealth and could not repair its canals. Effect: new Islamic states, most dominated by Turkic peoples, such as the Seljuks, Mamluks, and Delhi Sultanate.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did the Seljuks help cause the Crusades?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The textbook says the Seljuks limited Christian pilgrims' access to the Holy Land. Their victory at Manzikert (1071) also opened Anatolia to them, and the Byzantine emperor asked the Pope for help. Pope Urban II called the First Crusade in 1095, and the Crusaders took Jerusalem in 1099. Many accounts stress the Byzantine appeal and say stories of blocked pilgrims were exaggerated in Crusade preaching.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: What were the effects of the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The Abbasid Caliphate ended, and libraries including the House of Wisdom were destroyed. Cultural centers shifted to cities such as Cairo and Delhi. Islamic civilization survived: the Mongol Ilkhans later converted to Islam and supported scholars such as al-Tusi.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did merchants and Sufis spread Islam beyond lands Muslim armies conquered?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Islamic society respected merchants (Muhammad and his first wife, Khadija, were merchants), and some merchants were even sent out as missionaries. They carried Islam along trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean routes to West Africa (Mali), East Africa (Swahili coast), and Southeast Asia (Malacca). Sufis adapted to local cultures, so many people converted. Rulers and merchants often converted first, and Islam blended with local traditions.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: What made the Mamluk Sultanate rich, and what later weakened it?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The Mamluks controlled Egypt and the Red Sea spice trade and handled the cotton and sugar trade between the Islamic world and Europe. When the Portuguese and other Europeans opened new sea routes (after c. 1500), trade bypassed Egypt and Mamluk power declined; the Ottomans conquered them in 1517.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did learning from other cultures shape Islamic scholarship and Europe?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Following the saying "Go in quest of knowledge even unto China," Muslim scholars translated Greek works into Arabic, adopted Indian mathematics (including the numerals Europeans later called "Arabic"), and adopted papermaking from China. Through Muslims, Europeans gained Greek learning, Indian mathematics, and papermaking, which was vital to spreading ideas in Europe.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did translation in Toledo affect Christian Europe?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>After 1085, scholars such as Gerard of Cremona translated Arabic works (Aristotle, Ptolemy, Ibn Sina, Ibn Rushd) into Latin. Europe recovered Aristotle, Thomas Aquinas built scholasticism in the universities, and this learning later fed the Renaissance.</details>

## 5. Comparison & AP Writing

- [ ] **Compare: How were the Seljuks, the Mamluks, and the Delhi Sultanate similar and different?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Similar: all were Sunni, Turkic-led states that rose as the Abbasids fragmented and ruled diverse populations. Different: the Seljuks ruled as sultans in the caliph's name with Persian officials and reached almost to western China; the Mamluks were slave-soldiers who ruled Egypt without hereditary succession, defeated the Mongols, and grew rich on the Red Sea, cotton, and sugar trade; the Delhi Sultanate was a Muslim minority ruling a Hindu majority in India.</details>

- [ ] **Compare: How did political authority in Dar al-Islam change from the Abbasid Caliphate to its successor states?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Change: one caliph claiming to rule most of Dar al-Islam, with Arab and Persian leaders, gave way to many sultanates led by Turkic military elites. Their Turkic roots led on to the later Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires. Learning spread from Baghdad to many centers (Cairo, Córdoba, Bukhara, Maragha, Delhi). Continuity: sharia, Arabic scholarship, Persian administration, and respect for the caliph as a religious symbol.</details>

- [ ] **AP Short Answer: Explain one cause of the rise of new Islamic states after c. 1000.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** The fragmentation of the Abbasid Caliphate allowed new Turkic-led Islamic states to rise.<br/>
**Evidence:** Abbasid caliphs relied on Turkic slave-soldiers and could not control distant provinces, and as trade shifted north, Baghdad lost wealth. In 1055 the Seljuk Turks took Baghdad, and their leader ruled as sultan while the caliph kept only religious authority.<br/>
**Reasoning:** Because the caliph no longer had real military or economic power, Turkic military leaders could take political control while keeping the caliph as a religious figurehead.
</details>

- [ ] **AP Short Answer: Explain one way Islamic beliefs and practices affected society in Dar al-Islam from c. 1200 to c. 1450.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** Islamic law shaped how Muslim states treated Jews and Christians.<br/>
**Evidence:** As "People of the Book," Jews and Christians became dhimmi. They kept their religion, leaders, and courts but paid the jizya. Jewish scholars such as Maimonides thrived in Córdoba and Cairo.<br/>
**Reasoning:** Sharia gave non-Muslims protection in exchange for taxes, so diverse religious communities survived under Muslim rule, while the tax and social advantages encouraged gradual conversion to Islam.
</details>

- [ ] **AP Short Answer: Explain one way Islam affected the status of women in Dar al-Islam.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** Islam gave women some legal rights, but patriarchal limits remained and grew in cities.<br/>
**Evidence:** Women could own property, including a dowry paid to the bride rather than her father, could seek divorce under some conditions, and could remarry; Muhammad forbade female infanticide. But a woman's testimony counted for half of a man's, and veiling, seclusion, and the harem spread among urban elites.<br/>
**Reasoning:** Sharia protected women's property and family rights, so their legal position was stronger in some ways than before; yet as cities grew, social customs placed new limits on women's public lives.
</details>

- [ ] **AP Short Answer: Explain one effect of intellectual innovation in Dar al-Islam.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** Intellectual innovation in Dar al-Islam transferred knowledge to Christian Europe.<br/>
**Evidence:** The House of Wisdom translated Greek works into Arabic, and Ibn Rushd wrote commentaries on Aristotle that influenced Maimonides and then Thomas Aquinas. After 1085, scholars in Toledo translated these works into Latin.<br/>
**Reasoning:** Muslim scholars preserved and expanded ancient learning, so when it reached Europe through Spain, European thinkers such as Thomas Aquinas could use it to build scholasticism in the universities.
</details>

- [ ] **AP Long Essay Thesis: Evaluate the extent to which the fragmentation of the Abbasid Caliphate changed political authority in Dar al-Islam from c. 1200 to c. 1450.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** Fragmentation greatly changed who held political power, but Islamic religious and legal traditions continued.<br/>
**Evidence:** Turkic sultans replaced the caliph as real rulers: the Seljuks ruled as sultans after 1055, the Mamluks seized Egypt in 1250, and the Delhi Sultanate rose in 1206. However, these states kept sharia, relied on the ulama, and claimed legitimacy from a caliph, such as the Mamluks' "shadow caliph" in Cairo.<br/>
**Reasoning:** Military power moved to Turkic elites, but they still needed Islamic legitimacy to rule, so political authority changed while the religious foundations of government stayed the same.
</details>
