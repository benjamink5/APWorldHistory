## 1. Key Terms

- [ ] **What are push factors and pull factors? Give one example of each.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Push factors drive people to leave home; pull factors attract them to a new place. Example push: the Irish Potato Famine (1845–1852). Example pull: factory and railroad jobs in the United States.</details>

- [ ] **What is the difference between internal and external migration?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Internal migration is movement within one country, such as English farm workers moving to Manchester. External migration crosses borders or oceans, such as Italians moving to Argentina. The CED notes that both kinds of migrants increasingly moved to cities.</details>

- [ ] **What was the global urbanization of the 19th century?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The rapid growth of cities worldwide as internal and external migrants moved to them. By 1851 more than half of Britain's people lived in towns and cities, and port cities such as Buenos Aires, Singapore, and Calcutta also grew quickly.</details>

- [ ] **What is return migration, and why did it become more common after 1750?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Migrants going back to their home societies, periodically or permanently. It became more common because steamships and railroads made travel cheaper, faster, and regular.</details>

- [ ] **What are remittances?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Money that migrants send home to their families. Lebanese merchants in the Americas and Japanese plantation workers in Hawaii, for example, sent savings home to buy land and pay debts.</details>

- [ ] **What is chain migration?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Migration that follows family or village members who went earlier. Relatives abroad sent money for tickets and helped newcomers find housing and work.</details>

- [ ] **What is indentured servitude, and why is it called semicoerced?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A contract to work for a fixed time (often five years) in return for passage, food, housing, and a small wage. It was semicoerced because workers signed contracts but were often misled by recruiters, could not change employers, and could be punished for breaking the contract.</details>

- [ ] **What was the credit-ticket system?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A system in which a broker paid a Chinese migrant's fare to the United States and the migrant repaid it from wages. Most Chinese migrants to California came this way rather than under formal indenture.</details>

- [ ] **What was penal transportation?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Sending convicts to distant colonies to serve their sentences as forced labor. Britain sent about 160,000 convicts to Australia (1788–1868); France sent convicts to French Guiana and New Caledonia.</details>

- [ ] **Who were the golondrinas?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>"Swallows": Italian seasonal workers who crossed the Atlantic to Argentina every year. They harvested during the southern summer and went home for the Italian farming season, a clear example of return migration made possible by steamships.</details>

## 2. People

- [ ] **Why did so many Irish migrate to the United States in the mid-1800s, and what work did they do?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The Irish Potato Famine (1845–1852), poverty, and evictions pushed them out. In cities such as Boston and New York, Irish men built canals and railroads and worked on docks and in factories, and many Irish women worked as domestic servants.</details>

- [ ] **Why did British engineers and geologists go to South Asia and Africa?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>To fill skilled, well-paid jobs in the empire. In India they built railways (first passenger line 1853), telegraphs, and canals, and the Geological Survey of India (1851) searched for coal. In South Africa they worked in the diamond (Kimberley, 1867) and gold (Witwatersrand, 1886) mines. Many returned to Britain after their terms.</details>

- [ ] **Who were the Japanese agricultural workers in Hawaii, and why did many return?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Japanese farmers who went to Hawaii's sugar plantations on three-year government contracts from 1885. Meiji land taxes and the 1880s slump had left them in debt. Many planned to work, save, and return to Japan, and many did.</details>

- [ ] **Who were the Lebanese merchants in the Americas?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Migrants from Mount Lebanon in Ottoman Syria who left because of land pressure and the decline of the silk industry. They started as peddlers in the United States, Brazil, Argentina, and Mexico, then became shopkeepers and traders. Called "turcos" because of their Ottoman passports, many sent remittances home and some returned.</details>

- [ ] **What role did Chinese workers play in the American West?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>They came for the California Gold Rush and later built much of the Central Pacific Railroad in the 1860s. Most came under the credit-ticket system, repaying their fares from wages.</details>

## 3. Dates & Events

- [ ] **1788–1868**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Britain transported about 160,000 convicts to Australia, beginning with the First Fleet in 1788.</details>

- [ ] **1807**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Britain banned the Atlantic slave trade. Britain then abolished slavery in its empire in 1833 (in effect from 1834).</details>

- [ ] **1834**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The first Indian indentured workers reached Mauritius, as plantations looked for workers to replace freed slaves. The system lasted until 1917.</details>

- [ ] **1845–1852**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The Irish Potato Famine. Potato blight destroyed the main food crop; about 1 million people died and over 1 million emigrated.</details>

- [ ] **1849 and 1851**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The California Gold Rush (1849) and the Australian gold rush (1851) drew migrants from Europe, the Americas, and China.</details>

- [ ] **1885**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The first Japanese government-contract workers arrived in Hawaii to work on sugar plantations.</details>

- [ ] **1888**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Brazil abolished slavery, the last country in the Americas to do so. It had ended slave imports in 1850.</details>

## 4. Cause & Effect

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did steamships and railroads change migration?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>They made travel cheaper, faster, and regular. More poor people could migrate, more of them moved to port and rail cities, and many could return home periodically or permanently.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did population growth lead to migration?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Better food supply and new crops raised populations in Europe, China, India, and Japan. Farms were divided into smaller plots and land grew scarce, so many people left for cities or for other countries.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did an environmental disaster cause mass migration from Ireland?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Potato blight destroyed the crop most Irish families depended on. The resulting famine (1845–1852) killed about 1 million people and pushed over 1 million to emigrate, mainly to the United States.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did the abolition of slavery lead to indentured servitude?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>After Britain abolished slavery (1833), plantation owners in places such as Mauritius, British Guiana, and Trinidad lost their labor force. Indian and Chinese indentured servants were recruited to replace enslaved workers.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: Why did the new global capitalist economy keep using coerced labor?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Industrial countries wanted cheap sugar, cotton, coffee, guano, and minerals. Employers turned to the cheapest labor they could get: enslaved people in Brazil, Cuba, and the U.S. South, indentured servants in the Caribbean and Peru, and convicts in Australia.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: Why did Japanese farmers migrate to Hawaii?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Meiji land tax reform (1873) and the 1880s economic slump left many farmers in debt, while Hawaii's sugar planters needed workers and paid higher wages. The result was contract migration from 1885, with many workers later returning.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did gold discoveries shape migration?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Gold rushes in California (1849), Australia (1851), and South Africa (1886) drew huge numbers of free migrants. They created fast-growing, multiethnic cities such as San Francisco, Melbourne, and Johannesburg.</details>

## 5. Comparison & AP Writing

- [ ] **Compare: How did the migration of the Irish to the United States differ from the migration of Indian indentured servants?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Similar: both were driven by poverty and famine. Different: the Irish were free migrants who paid their own way and could change jobs; Indian indentured servants had their passage paid in exchange for a fixed contract, were bound to one employer, and went mostly to British plantation colonies.</details>

- [ ] **Compare: How were Indian and Chinese indentured servitude similar and different?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Similar: both replaced enslaved labor after abolition and supplied plantations in the global capitalist economy. Different: the British colonial government organized and regulated Indian indenture (1834–1917, to Mauritius, the Caribbean, Natal, and Fiji), while private brokers ran the Chinese coolie trade to Cuba and Peru (1847–1874), often with kidnapping and fraud.</details>

- [ ] **Compare: How was indentured servitude different from slavery?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Enslaved people were property for life and their children were born enslaved. Indentured servants signed a contract for a fixed term, received a small wage, and could become free or return home afterward. Both systems, however, used harsh discipline and served plantation economies.</details>

- [ ] **AP Short Answer: Explain one environmental factor that caused migration between 1750 and 1900.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** Famine caused by crop disease forced large numbers of people to migrate.<br/>
**Evidence:** During the Irish Potato Famine (1845–1852), potato blight destroyed Ireland's main food crop; about 1 million people died and over 1 million emigrated, mostly to the United States.<br/>
**Reasoning:** When an environmental disaster destroyed the food supply in a society already under population pressure, people could no longer survive at home, so emigration became the main way to escape starvation.
</details>

- [ ] **AP Short Answer: Explain one way indentured servitude supported the global capitalist economy from 1750 to 1900.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** Indentured servitude supplied cheap plantation labor after the abolition of slavery.<br/>
**Evidence:** After Britain abolished slavery in 1833, more than 1 million Indians went under contract to Mauritius, British Guiana, Trinidad, Natal, and Fiji, and Chinese workers were shipped to Cuba and Peru (1847–1874).<br/>
**Reasoning:** These semicoerced workers kept sugar and guano production going for industrial markets at low cost, so the global economy continued to rely on unfree labor even after slavery ended.
</details>

- [ ] **AP Long Essay Thesis: Evaluate the relative importance of environmental and economic factors in causing migration from 1750 to 1900.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** Environmental and demographic pressures often pushed people to leave, but economic factors were more important because they determined where and how migrants went.<br/>
**Evidence:** Population growth and famines such as the Irish Potato Famine pushed millions from Ireland, China, and India. Yet jobs in factories, mines, railroads, and gold rushes pulled free migrants to the Americas and Australia, and plantation demand after abolition created Indian and Chinese indentured servitude.<br/>
**Reasoning:** Environmental pressure explains why people left, but the needs of the new global capitalist economy, served by steamships and railroads, shaped the destinations, the forms of labor (free, semicoerced, coerced), and even the chance to return.
</details>
