## 1. Key Terms

- [ ] **What is an ethnic enclave?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A neighborhood or community where one migrant group lives together and keeps its own language, religion, food, and institutions. Examples: San Francisco's Chinatown, New York's Little Italy, Irish Catholic parishes in Boston, the Indian community of Durban in Natal.</details>

- [ ] **What are remittances, and why did they matter to home societies?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Money that migrants send back to their families. Many families sent one man abroad so his wages could support the household at home, and women in the home society often managed this money.</details>

- [ ] **Who were the "white widows" (vedove bianche)?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Wives in southern Italy whose husbands had emigrated to the Americas, often for years. They farmed the family land, hired workers, managed remittances, bought land, and dealt with officials.</details>

- [ ] **What was a "bachelor society"?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A mostly male migrant community, such as early Chinatowns in the United States. It formed because most migrants were men and because laws such as the Page Act (1875) kept Chinese women out.</details>

- [ ] **What were the Chinese Six Companies?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>An alliance of Chinese district associations (huiguan) in San Francisco. It gave immigrants aid, settled disputes, hired lawyers, and lobbied against anti-Chinese laws.</details>

- [ ] **Who were the "passenger Indians"?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Indian merchants, mostly Gujarati, who paid their own way to Natal and East Africa after indentured laborers had arrived. They opened shops and became the target of white settlers' resentment.</details>

- [ ] **What is nativism?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Hostility toward immigrants by people who see themselves as the "native" population. In the 1800s it targeted Irish and Italian Catholics on religious and ethnic grounds and Asians on racial grounds.</details>

- [ ] **What was the Know-Nothing Party?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>An anti-Catholic, anti-immigrant U.S. political party of the 1850s (officially the American Party). It wanted to limit the political power of immigrants, especially Irish Catholics.</details>

- [ ] **What was a head tax, and which state used one against Chinese immigrants?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A fee charged to each immigrant of a particular group. Canada's Chinese Immigration Act (1885) charged each Chinese immigrant $50, raised to $100 (1900) and $500 (1903).</details>

- [ ] **What was the dictation test in the White Australia policy?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>An entry test under the Immigration Restriction Act (1901). An officer could make an immigrant write 50 words in any European language he chose, so officials could exclude non-Europeans without naming race in the law.</details>

## 2. People

- [ ] **Who was Mohandas Gandhi, and what did he do in Natal?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A young Indian lawyer who arrived in Natal in 1893. After being thrown off a first-class train compartment at Pietermaritzburg because he was Indian, he helped found the Natal Indian Congress (1894) to fight laws on Indians' voting rights and taxes. His later satyagraha campaigns (from 1906) shaped his leadership in India.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Denis Kearney?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The leader of the Workingmen's Party in California in the late 1870s, known for the slogan "The Chinese must go!" He blamed Chinese workers for low wages and helped build pressure for the Chinese Exclusion Act.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Emma Lazarus, and why is her poem useful for this topic?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>An American poet who wrote "The New Colossus" (1883), with the lines "Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free." It was written one year after the Chinese Exclusion Act, which shows that the U.S. both welcomed and excluded immigrants.</details>

- [ ] **Which U.S. president signed the Chinese Exclusion Act?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Chester A. Arthur, in 1882.</details>

- [ ] **Who was Wong Kim Ark?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>A man born in San Francisco to Chinese parents. In United States v. Wong Kim Ark (1898), the Supreme Court ruled that he was a U.S. citizen by birth, even though his parents could not become citizens under the Chinese Exclusion Act.</details>

## 3. Dates & Events

- [ ] **1845–1852**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The Great Famine in Ireland. About one million Irish emigrated, mostly to the United States and Canada, and built Catholic enclaves in cities such as Boston and New York.</details>

- [ ] **1860**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Indian indentured laborers began arriving in Natal to work on sugar plantations. Their community, joined by passenger Indians, became the largest Indian community in Africa.</details>

- [ ] **1875**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The U.S. Page Act made it very hard for Chinese women to enter the country, which helped make Chinatowns into bachelor societies.</details>

- [ ] **1882**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The U.S. Chinese Exclusion Act banned Chinese laborers for 10 years and barred Chinese from citizenship. It was the first major U.S. law to ban immigration by a specific ethnic or national group.</details>

- [ ] **1885**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Canada's Chinese Immigration Act set a $50 head tax on Chinese immigrants. In the same year, white miners killed Chinese miners in the Rock Springs Massacre in Wyoming.</details>

- [ ] **1894**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Gandhi helped found the Natal Indian Congress to defend Indians' rights in Natal.</details>

- [ ] **1901**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>The new Commonwealth of Australia passed the Immigration Restriction Act, with its dictation test. This law was the core of the White Australia policy.</details>

## 4. Cause & Effect

- [ ] **Cause: Why were most migrants men?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Employers wanted young male workers for mines, railroads, plantations, and docks; families sent one man abroad to earn remittances; and some laws (the Page Act) and recruiting practices kept women out.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did male migration change women's roles in home societies?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Women took on roles men had held. In southern Italy, "white widows" farmed and managed money; in southern China, wives ran households and managed remittances; in India, women worked the fields and did wage labor. The changes gave women more economic responsibility but were often temporary.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: Why did migrants form ethnic enclaves, and what did enclaves do?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Migrants faced a new language, low-paid work, and hostility, so they settled near people from home. Enclaves gave housing, jobs, loans, and protection, and they transplanted culture through temples and churches, food, newspapers, festivals, and mutual-aid societies.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did job competition lead to anti-Chinese laws in the U.S.?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>After the railroads were finished, many Chinese workers looked for other jobs, and white workers blamed them for low wages. Groups like Kearney's Workingmen's Party and violence like the Rock Springs Massacre built pressure for the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882).</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did Social Darwinism and scientific racism shape immigration policy?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>They ranked peoples in a racial hierarchy (see 6.1). States therefore welcomed European immigrants but restricted Asians by race, as in the Chinese Exclusion Act, Canada's head tax, and the White Australia policy.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: Why did Brazil recruit Italian workers after 1888?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Brazil abolished slavery in 1888, so São Paulo coffee planters needed new workers. The state paid passages for Italians, who formed large communities in and around São Paulo.</details>

- [ ] **Cause → Effect: How did Natal's anti-Indian laws lead to organized resistance?**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Natal taxed formerly indentured Indians, kept most Indians from voting, and imposed an entry test (1895–1897). Gandhi and the Natal Indian Congress (1894) organized petitions and protests, which grew into his satyagraha campaigns.</details>

## 5. Comparison & AP Writing

- [ ] **Compare: the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) and the White Australia policy (1901).**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Similarities: both restricted immigration by race, reflected Social Darwinist ideas, and answered white workers' fears of cheap Asian labor. Differences: the U.S. law openly named the Chinese, while Australia used a dictation test in a European language so race was not written into the law; the U.S. law targeted Chinese laborers, while Australia's policy targeted all non-Europeans.</details>

- [ ] **Compare: how U.S. society received Irish immigrants and Chinese immigrants.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Both faced prejudice and violence. The Irish faced religious and ethnic nativism (the Know-Nothings, the Philadelphia Bible Riots) but could become citizens, vote, and build political machines. The Chinese faced racial exclusion: they were barred from citizenship and, after 1882, from entry as laborers.</details>

- [ ] **Compare: Italian migrants in the United States and in Argentina.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>Similarities: both built enclaves (Little Italy in New York, La Boca in Buenos Aires) with Catholic parishes, mutual-aid societies, and Italian newspapers, and many migrants returned home. Differences: in the U.S., Italians faced prejudice and violence (New Orleans, 1891), while Argentina's state actively recruited European immigrants, and Italians shaped Argentine speech, food, and the tango.</details>

- [ ] **AP SAQ: Explain one way migration changed gender roles in home societies, 1750–1900.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** Because most migrants were men, women in home societies took on economic roles that men had held.<br/>
**Evidence:** In southern Italy, the "white widows" of men who went to the United States and Argentina farmed the family land, hired workers, managed remittances, and bought land.<br/>
**Reasoning:** With husbands gone for years, someone had to run the farm and the family's money, so women gained new economic responsibility, even though their legal status usually did not change.<br/>
</details>

- [ ] **AP SAQ: Explain one way a state responded to increased immigration, 1750–1900.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** The United States responded to Chinese immigration by restricting it by race.<br/>
**Evidence:** The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) banned Chinese laborers for 10 years and barred Chinese from citizenship; it was renewed in 1892 and made permanent in 1902.<br/>
**Reasoning:** White workers blamed Chinese labor for low wages after the railroads were finished, and racial ideas such as Social Darwinism made exclusion seem acceptable, so the state used border control to keep out a group it saw as racially undesirable.<br/>
</details>

- [ ] **AP LEQ thesis: Evaluate the extent to which migration changed receiving societies from 1750 to 1900.**
<details><summary>▶ Answer</summary>
**Claim:** Migration significantly changed receiving societies by creating ethnic enclaves that transplanted new cultures, but it also hardened racial hierarchies, as states responded with prejudice and new border controls.<br/>
**Evidence:** Chinese, Indian, Irish, and Italian migrants built enclaves such as San Francisco's Chinatown, the Indian community of Natal, Irish parishes in Boston, and Italian La Boca in Buenos Aires; at the same time, the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882), Canada's head tax (1885), Natal's laws, and the White Australia policy (1901) restricted Asian immigrants by race.<br/>
**Reasoning:** Migrants changed the culture, labor force, and politics of receiving societies, but those societies welcomed European migrants more than Asian ones, so migration both diversified them and pushed states to define citizenship and borders by race.<br/>
</details>
