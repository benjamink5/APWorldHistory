## Part 2. Detailed Explanations

## Topic 4.1: Technological Innovations

### Question 1: The Magnetic Compass in European Navigation — Answer (C)

**Correct answer:** (C) The diffusion into Europe of a navigational technology that originated in China

**Explanation:** Neckham describes sailors using a magnetized needle to find north in cloudy weather or at night. The magnetic compass was first used for navigation in China (Song dynasty) and became known in Europe around the 12th century. CED 4.1 emphasizes that technology from the Classical, Islamic, and Asian worlds diffused into Europe and laid the groundwork for transoceanic voyages. The key skill is sourcing: connecting the 12th-century date of the source to the origin of the technology.

**CED connection:** Cross-cultural interaction diffused Chinese, Islamic, and Classical technologies (compass, astrolabe, lateen sail) into Europe, enabling later transoceanic voyages.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** The independent invention of the magnetic compass by Renaissance-era Portuguese navigators — Neckham lived in the 12th century, long before the Renaissance and the Portuguese Age of Exploration, and the compass originated in China.
- **(B)** The replacement of magnetic instruments by astronomical charts in European navigation — The source describes a magnetic needle, not astronomical charts. Charts supplemented the compass rather than replacing it.
- **(D)** The transfer of Islamic legal scholarship into Christian Europe through Al-Andalus — Knowledge did pass through Al-Andalus, but the source concerns a navigational instrument, not legal scholarship.

---

### Question 2: The Lateen Sail — Answer (B)

**Correct answer:** (B) allowing ships to sail across or against prevailing winds rather than only with them

**Explanation:** The passage explains that square sails could only be used with the wind, whereas the triangular lateen sail could catch the wind on either side of the ship and move in different directions. This made tacking against headwinds and crosswinds possible. The distractors attribute functions of other 4.1 technologies (astrolabe, fluyt, carrack) to the lateen sail, testing whether students can distinguish each technology's purpose.

**CED connection:** The lateen sail, used by Arab and Indian Ocean sailors, let ships tack against the wind; combined with square sails it made open-ocean voyages feasible.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** allowing navigators to determine their latitude by measuring the angle of the sun — Measuring latitude by the sun's angle is the function of the astrolabe and astronomical charts.
- **(C)** increasing cargo capacity so that merchants could lower shipping costs — Maximizing cargo and lowering shipping costs describe the Dutch fluyt.
- **(D)** providing stable platforms for mounting heavy cannon on oceangoing ships — Providing platforms for heavy cannon describes the large carrack.

---

### Question 3: The Dutch Fluyt — Answer (A)

**Correct answer:** (A) The Dutch Republic's emergence as Europe's leading commercial carrier in the seventeenth century

**Explanation:** The fluyt reduced armament and enlarged cargo space so that a small crew could operate it, making freight rates very low. This allowed the Dutch to dominate European shipping and carrying trade in the 17th century and supported the success of the VOC. The question tests the causal chain: ship design → lower costs → commercial advantage.

**CED connection:** The fluyt's low operating cost made the Dutch the 'middlemen of Europe' and underpinned the commercial success of the VOC.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(B)** Portugal's success in becoming the first European state to reach India by sea — Portugal reached India in 1498 using 15th-century caravels and carracks; the fluyt was a late-16th-century Dutch design.
- **(C)** The Ming dynasty's decision to end state-sponsored maritime expeditions — The Ming ended maritime expeditions in the 1430s for domestic political reasons unrelated to European ship design.
- **(D)** Spain's conquest of the Aztec and Inca empires in the early sixteenth century — Both the date (1520s–1530s) and the country are wrong, and conquest is not directly related to cargo-ship design.

---

### Question 4: Al-Andalus and Cross-Cultural Knowledge — Answer (C)

**Correct answer:** (C) Knowledge preserved and expanded in the Islamic world contributed to later European innovation

**Explanation:** The textbook explains that Europeans combined classical Greek star navigation with new knowledge from Islamic and Asian sailors and scholars, and that Al-Andalus (Islamic Spain) was where Islamic ideas diffused into Europe. The astrolabe, refined by 12th-century Muslim navigators, is a prime example. This targets the core of CED 4.1: diffusion of technology through cross-cultural interaction.

**CED connection:** Islamic scholars preserved Greek learning and refined tools like the astrolabe; Al-Andalus transmitted this knowledge to Christian Europe.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** Iberian rulers banned contact with Islamic scholars to protect Christian orthodoxy — Al-Andalus is described as a channel through which Islamic ideas entered Europe, contradicting a ban on contact.
- **(B)** European navigational advances resulted mainly from isolation from Afro-Eurasian trade networks — The source stresses the opposite—cross-cultural interaction through trade networks, not isolation.
- **(D)** Classical Greek knowledge was lost in Europe until it was rediscovered in the Americas — Classical knowledge was preserved and transmitted through the Islamic world and Byzantium, not rediscovered in the Americas.

### Question 5: Push Factors for European Expansion — Answer (A)

**Correct answer:** (A) Social and economic pressures within Europe encouraged many people to seek opportunities abroad

**Explanation:** The source cites shortages of work and food caused by population growth, primogeniture laws that gave estates only to eldest sons, and religious minorities seeking tolerance. These are all internal 'push factors' showing that social and economic pressures, not just technology, encouraged overseas migration.

**CED connection:** Population growth, primogeniture, religious dissent, and desire for adventure were 'push factors' behind European exploration and settlement.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(B)** A labor shortage in Europe forced states to recruit colonists from Asia — The passage describes overpopulation and a lack of work, the opposite of a labor shortage.
- **(C)** European monarchs prohibited younger sons of nobles from leaving their estates — Primogeniture excluded younger sons from land, which actually encouraged them to go abroad.
- **(D)** Religious minorities were welcomed in Europe and had little reason to emigrate — The passage says religious minorities left in search of tolerant places to settle.

### Question 6: Caravel and Carrack — Answer (D)

**Correct answer:** (D) the caravel suited coastal exploration, while the carrack suited long-distance armed trade

**Explanation:** The caravel was small, fast, and shallow-drafted, ideal for exploring unknown coastlines; the carrack's large hull and cannon platforms suited long-distance armed trade. The question compares the purposes of the three ship types named in CED 4.1 (caravel, carrack, fluyt).

**CED connection:** Caravel = maneuverable explorer; carrack = large armed cargo carrier; fluyt = cheap Dutch freighter (CED 4.1 ship designs).

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** the caravel replaced the lateen sail with square sails to increase speed — The caravel did not abandon the lateen sail; it combined lateen and square sails.
- **(B)** both ships were developed by the Dutch to compete with the Hanseatic League — Both ships were Iberian (Portuguese/Spanish); the Dutch ship type was the fluyt.
- **(C)** the carrack was designed mainly for exploring shallow rivers and coastlines — Shallow-draft coastal and river exploration describes the caravel; the carrack was a large oceangoing ship.

## Topic 4.2: Exploration: Causes and Events

### Question 7: Columbus and Royal Sponsorship — Answer (D)

**Correct answer:** (D) Transoceanic voyages were costly and risky enough that state backing was usually essential

**Explanation:** The textbook explains that voyages like Columbus's were so expensive that, without state financing, they would have been beyond the reach of explorers and most merchants. In return, monarchs sought territory, wealth, taxes, and converts. This directly addresses the CED 4.2 objective on the role of states in maritime exploration.

**CED connection:** State sponsorship (GOV) was central to exploration: voyages were too expensive for most merchants, and rulers sought wealth, territory, and converts.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** Iberian exploration was financed chiefly by private joint-stock companies — Joint-stock companies were a 17th-century English and Dutch feature; Spain and Portugal relied on direct royal investment.
- **(B)** European monarchs supported exploration mainly to establish religious toleration — The Spanish crown pursued Catholic expansion and religious uniformity (e.g., the 1492 expulsion of Jews), not toleration.
- **(C)** The papacy directly funded and commanded most Atlantic expeditions — The papacy mediated (e.g., the Treaty of Tordesillas), but monarchs financed and directed the voyages.

---

### Question 8: Mercantilism — Answer (C)

**Correct answer:** (C) seek colonies that supplied raw materials and bought the home country's manufactured goods

**Explanation:** Mercantilism treated gold and silver reserves as the measure of national wealth and sought to maximize exports and minimize imports to bring bullion in. Colonies served as sources of raw materials and markets for the home country's manufactured goods, supported by tariffs, monopolies, and strong government intervention.

**CED connection:** Mercantilism equated national wealth with bullion and used colonies, tariffs, and monopolies to achieve a favorable balance of trade.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** spend its bullion reserves to purchase luxury goods from rival states — Draining bullion reserves is exactly what mercantilism tried to prevent.
- **(B)** lower tariffs to encourage imports of foreign manufactured goods — Encouraging imports directly contradicts the mercantilist principle of buying as little as possible from others.
- **(D)** abolish royal trade monopolies in favor of unregulated free markets — Mercantilism strengthened state intervention and monopolies; free trade was advocated later (e.g., Adam Smith).

---

### Question 9: The Portuguese Trading-Post Empire — Answer (D)

**Correct answer:** (D) sought to control sea lanes through fortified ports rather than conquer large inland territories

**Explanation:** Portugal built forts at maritime choke points such as Hormuz, Goa, and Malacca and required ships to buy permits (cartaz) to control the spice trade. This contrasts with Spain's territorial empire, which conquered the Aztecs and Incas and ruled large lands and populations directly. This is a comparison-skill question.

**CED connection:** Trading-post empire: control of strategic choke points and a permit (cartaz) system, not territorial conquest.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** relied on large numbers of European settlers to farm Asian lands — Portugal's small population made large-scale settlement impossible, so it relied on fortified outposts.
- **(B)** acted mainly as agents of the Ming dynasty's tribute system — Portugal pursued its own trade monopoly; it was not an agent of the Ming.
- **(C)** avoided the use of naval force and depended on diplomatic marriages — Naval force was central (e.g., the Battle of Diu), and the permit system was enforced by arms.

### Question 10: Spanish Manila — Answer (B)

**Correct answer:** (B) The exchange of American silver for Chinese silk and porcelain across the Pacific

**Explanation:** From Manila, Spain sent annual galleons carrying silver from Acapulco to exchange with Chinese merchants for silk and porcelain. This created the first sustained trans-Pacific trade and, combined with China's silver demand (Single Whip tax), helped integrate the world economy. Note: the textbook says Spain 'annexed' the Philippines in 1521, but 1521 marks Magellan's arrival and claim; actual colonization began in 1565.

**CED connection:** The Manila Galleons (Acapulco–Manila) linked American silver to Chinese markets, creating the first sustained trans-Pacific trade.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** The rapid spread of Protestant Christianity throughout Southeast Asia — Catholicism, not Protestantism, spread in the Philippines—and that is unrelated to Manila's commercial role.
- **(C)** A Spanish monopoly over the spice trade that displaced the Dutch East India Company — The spice trade was led by Portugal and later the Dutch (VOC); Spain never displaced the VOC.
- **(D)** The first Spanish sea route to Asia around the southern tip of Africa — The route around Africa was Portugal's; Spain used the Pacific (Acapulco–Manila).

### Question 11: Northern Atlantic Crossings — Answer (B)

**Correct answer:** (B) Southern sea routes to Asia were dominated by Portuguese and Spanish claims and naval power

**Explanation:** Under the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494), Spain and Portugal divided the non-European world: Portugal controlled the route around Africa and Spain the Atlantic–Pacific route. Latecomers England, France, and the Netherlands searched for a Northwest Passage and, in the process, colonized North America (Quebec, Jamestown, New Amsterdam).

**CED connection:** Latecomers (England, France, Netherlands) searched for a Northwest Passage because Iberian powers controlled the known southern routes.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** The Ottoman Empire had opened free overland passage for European merchants — The Ottomans controlled gateways to Mediterranean and overland trade, which was itself a reason to seek new routes.
- **(C)** Asian demand for European manufactured goods had collapsed by 1500 — Europe actually lacked goods Asians wanted and paid in silver; there is no evidence of a collapse in demand.
- **(D)** The Treaty of Tordesillas had granted northern European states exclusive rights in Asia — Tordesillas divided the world between Spain and Portugal and granted no rights to northern European states.

### Question 12: Jesuits in China — Answer (D)

**Correct answer:** (D) gain influence by appealing to the intellectual interests of China's ruling elite

**Explanation:** Jesuits such as Matteo Ricci and Adam Schall von Bell used astronomy, mathematics, and cartography to attract the interest of the court elite—a top-down conversion strategy. Resistance from the scholar-gentry kept conversions few. The final sentence about failure is a trap: the question asks about the strategy's intent, not its outcome.

**CED connection:** Jesuits used science, astronomy, and cultural accommodation to approach the Chinese court, but scholar-gentry resistance limited conversions.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** replace Confucian civil service exams with European university degrees — The Jesuits did not try to replace the Confucian system; they tried to accommodate Confucian culture.
- **(B)** establish Macau as an independent Christian kingdom free of imperial control — Macau was a Portuguese trading post; the Jesuits' goal was converting the court elite.
- **(C)** convert the Chinese population through military conquest supported by Portugal — The Jesuits used learning and science, not force.

## Topic 4.3: Columbian Exchange

### Question 13: An Aztec Lament — Answer (A)

**Correct answer:** (A) The devastation of indigenous societies following Spanish conquest and epidemic disease

**Explanation:** The poem records the destruction of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco after 1521 from the perspective of the conquered. Superior weapons and especially epidemic diseases such as smallpox caused indigenous societies to collapse. AP frequently pairs a source's point of view with its historical situation.

**CED connection:** Conquest and Old World diseases shattered indigenous American societies; Aztec poetry records this catastrophe from the perspective of the conquered.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(B)** The blending of Aztec and Catholic traditions into a new syncretic faith — Religious syncretism (e.g., the Virgin of Guadalupe) came later; the poem is about loss and grief.
- **(C)** The successful expulsion of Spanish forces from the Valley of Mexico — The Aztecs were conquered with the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521; the poem laments ruin, not victory.
- **(D)** Aztec grief over the collapse of long-distance trade with the Inca Empire — There is no evidence of long-distance Aztec–Inca trade; the poem concerns the destruction of Mexico itself.

---

### Question 14: The Great Dying — Answer (D)

**Correct answer:** (D) Indigenous Americans lacked acquired immunity to diseases such as smallpox and measles

**Explanation:** After millennia of isolation, indigenous Americans had never been exposed to Afro-Eurasian pathogens and had no immunity. Smallpox, measles, influenza, and malaria killed 50–90% of populations within a century. 'Weapons' is the most tempting distractor, but both the source and the CED identify disease as the main cause.

**CED connection:** Isolation meant no immunity; smallpox, measles, influenza, and malaria caused the 'Great Dying' of 50–90% of native populations.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** European crops such as wheat replaced maize and caused widespread famine — Maize continued to be grown, and there is no evidence famine was the main cause of population decline.
- **(B)** Large numbers of indigenous people migrated voluntarily to Europe and Africa — There was no large voluntary migration; instead, Africans were forcibly brought to the Americas.
- **(C)** Most indigenous deaths resulted from battles fought against Spanish firearms — The passage states that weapons aided conquest but disease caused the majority of deaths.

### Question 15: American Crops in Europe — Answer (A)

**Correct answer:** (A) significant population growth in parts of Europe and other regions of Afro-Eurasia

**Explanation:** Potatoes and maize produced high calories per acre and grew in poor soil, driving European population growth in the 16th–17th centuries. China (sweet potatoes, maize) and Africa (manioc, peanuts) saw similar effects. CED: 'Populations in Afro-Eurasia benefited nutritionally from the increased diversity of American food crops.'

**CED connection:** High-calorie American crops (potato, maize, manioc, sweet potato) fueled population growth in Europe, China, and Africa.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(B)** the spread of smallpox from the Americas to Europe — Smallpox spread in the opposite direction, from Europe to the Americas.
- **(C)** a reduction in Europe's demand for enslaved labor in the Americas — The new crops did not reduce demand for enslaved labor, which was tied to sugar and tobacco plantations.
- **(D)** a decline in European agricultural output as farmers abandoned grain crops — Potatoes and maize greatly increased calorie supply rather than reducing output.

### Question 16: The Horse on the Great Plains — Answer (C)

**Correct answer:** (C) Greater food surpluses alongside intensified competition among Plains peoples

**Explanation:** The horse let Plains peoples hunt buffalo efficiently over wide areas and produce food surpluses. At the same time, groups with more horses gained more power, intensifying competition and conflict. The question tests complex causation: one cause producing both positive and negative effects.

**CED connection:** Domesticated animals from Europe transformed American societies—horses reshaped Plains hunting economies, politics, and warfare.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** The immediate conquest of the Plains by Spanish cavalry — The passage concerns changes within indigenous societies; Spanish conquest of the Plains is not mentioned.
- **(B)** A decline in warfare as tribes cooperated to share herds — The passage says competition and armed conflict increased.
- **(D)** A shift from hunting to settled maize agriculture among Plains peoples — The horse made buffalo hunting more efficient, reinforcing a mobile hunting life rather than settled farming.

### Question 17: Population Growth in Africa — Answer (A)

**Correct answer:** (A) Manioc (cassava) and maize brought to Africa from the Americas

**Explanation:** The textbook explains that despite the slave trade, Africa's population grew in the 16th–17th centuries because of nutritious crops such as manioc (cassava) from Brazil (Topic 4.5 also mentions maize and peanuts). The rice-and-okra option is a classic AP trap: true but irrelevant. Note: the textbook says yams and manioc came from Brazil, but yams are native to Africa; the accurate American examples are manioc, maize, and peanuts.

**CED connection:** American crops such as manioc, maize, and peanuts thrived in African soils and partially offset population losses from the slave trade.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(B)** Sugarcane grown on plantations for export markets — Sugarcane was an export cash crop, not a food crop that increased Africa's population.
- **(C)** Wheat and barley introduced to West Africa from Europe — Wheat and barley are not the American crops the textbook describes and did not become staples in tropical West Africa.
- **(D)** Rice and okra, which enslaved Africans carried to the Americas — True, but the direction is reversed (Africa → Americas), so it cannot explain population growth in Africa.

### Question 18: Environmental Effects of the Exchange — Answer (C)

**Correct answer:** (C) It transformed American landscapes through plantation agriculture and livestock grazing

**Explanation:** The chart shows deforestation for sugar and wheat, soil depletion from repeated monoculture, and overgrazing and erosion caused by cattle, sheep, and goats. The CED theme Humans and the Environment (ENV) stresses the interaction in which environments shape societies and societies reshape environments. This is a chart-interpretation question.

**CED connection:** Old World crops and animals reshaped New World ecosystems: deforestation, monoculture soil depletion, overgrazing, erosion (Theme ENV).

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** It spread American livestock throughout Afro-Eurasia — The chart covers Eastern-to-Western Hemisphere transfers; livestock moved from Eurasia to the Americas.
- **(B)** It had significant demographic effects but few environmental consequences — The chart directly lists environmental consequences: deforestation, soil depletion, overgrazing, and erosion.
- **(D)** It reduced pressure on American forests by introducing more productive crops — The chart says deforestation occurred.

### Question 19: African Culture in the Americas — Answer (B)

**Correct answer:** (B) Enslaved Africans adapted their traditions to create new cultural forms in the Americas

**Explanation:** Forced migration mixed different language groups, but enslaved people combined European vocabulary with West African grammar to create creole languages (e.g., Gullah) and continued musical traditions such as the banjo and spirituals. This shows adaptation and synthesis rather than erasure. The first distractor exaggerates a partial truth (many languages were lost).

**CED connection:** Diaspora cultures blended African and European elements: creole languages (Gullah), music (banjo, spirituals), foods (gumbo).

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** Colonial governments required enslaved people to speak creole languages — Creole languages were created by enslaved people to communicate, not imposed by governments.
- **(C)** African cultures were completely erased within one generation of arrival in the Americas — The textbook states African cultures were not completely lost (creole languages, music, food).
- **(D)** Africans in the Americas adopted European music without modification — African musical traditions such as the banjo and syncopated rhythms were preserved and transformed.

## Topic 4.4: Maritime Empires Established

### Question 20: A West African Griot Song — Answer (C)

**Correct answer:** (C) The participation of some African rulers in seizing people to be enslaved

**Explanation:** In the song, the 'king' seizes people's property and wakes the sleeper to declare, 'You are not free anymore.' This reflects how some African rulers raided neighboring villages and sold captives to European traders (e.g., Dahomey). Textbook: 'The appalling shock of free people being seized and enslaved is captured in the griot song.'

**CED connection:** Some coastal African rulers captured and sold people to European traders in exchange for guns and goods, reshaping African states.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** The resistance of West African kingdoms to all European commercial contact — The song depicts a king taking his people's freedom, not resisting contact with Europeans.
- **(B)** The replacement of millet and peanuts by sugarcane plantations in West Africa — Sugar plantations expanded in the Americas; the song is about loss of freedom, not crop replacement.
- **(D)** The conversion of West African farmers to Christianity by Portuguese missionaries — The song contains nothing about religion or missionaries.

### Question 21: Tokugawa Japan's Restrictions — Answer (B)

**Correct answer:** (B) limit destabilizing foreign influence while keeping a tightly controlled channel of trade

**Explanation:** The Japanese government viewed the spread of Christianity as a threat to social order and political authority, banning Christian worship in 1587 and expelling foreigners and banning travel abroad in the 1630s. However, Dutch merchants at Deshima in Nagasaki and Chinese trade were permitted. 'Complete isolation' is the most common trap. Note: the 1587 ban was issued by Toyotomi Hideyoshi, before the Tokugawa shogunate was established (1603).

**CED connection:** Tokugawa 'Closed Country' (Sakoku) policies restricted Christianity and foreigners but preserved limited Dutch and Chinese trade.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** end all contact with the outside world, including all foreign trade — Dutch (Deshima) and Chinese trade continued, so ending 'all' trade is incorrect.
- **(C)** respond to a Spanish military conquest of the Japanese islands — Spain never conquered Japan.
- **(D)** imitate European mercantilism by founding overseas colonies — Japan actually prohibited its people from traveling abroad.

### Question 22: Ming Trade Restrictions — Answer (D)

**Correct answer:** (D) Tokugawa Japan's restrictions on foreign religion and trade

**Explanation:** After Zheng He's voyages, the Ming banned private overseas trade (Haijin), destroyed shipyards, and reinforced Confucian order. CED 4.4 lists the Ming and Tokugawa Japan together as Asian states that adopted restrictive or isolationist trade policies. All other options are trade-expansion policies, making this a similarity comparison.

**CED connection:** Some Asian states (Ming China's Haijin, Tokugawa Japan's Closed Country Edict) adopted restrictive or isolationist trade policies (CED 4.4).

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** Spain's sponsorship of trans-Pacific galleon voyages — Sponsoring the Manila galleons promoted long-distance trade.
- **(B)** The Dutch Republic's creation of a joint-stock trading company — Founding the VOC was a policy to actively expand overseas trade.
- **(C)** Portugal's construction of fortified trading posts in the Indian Ocean — Portugal's fortified posts were a policy of trade expansion, the opposite of Ming restriction.

### Question 23: Benin Art and the Portuguese — Answer (B)

**Correct answer:** (B) cultural interaction between African states and European traders

**Explanation:** Sixteenth-century Benin artisans depicted Portuguese soldiers and merchants on bronze plaques and sculptures. This shows that Europeans did not conquer Africa but traded with and influenced local kingdoms through contact. The question tests using art as historical evidence (sourcing).

**CED connection:** Coastal African states engaged Europeans largely on their own terms; art like the Benin bronzes records these encounters.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** the spread of Islamic artistic prohibitions into West African kingdoms — Figurative sculpture is unrelated to Islamic prohibitions on images.
- **(C)** the Portuguese conquest and direct rule of the Kingdom of Benin — Benin was not conquered by Portugal; Europeans traded with local rulers along the coast.
- **(D)** Benin's isolation from Atlantic trade networks — Depicting Europeans is itself evidence of contact with Atlantic trade networks.

### Question 24: Continuity in Indian Ocean Trade — Answer (C)

**Correct answer:** (C) European entry altered some trade patterns, but Asian and African merchant networks remained vital

**Explanation:** This is a continuity-and-change (CCOT) question. Europeans (Portuguese forts, the VOC) restructured some trade, but Swahili Arab, Omani, Gujarati, and Javanese merchants continued to flourish in intra-Asian trade. Claiming that Europeans took 'full' control is a classic AP exaggeration.

**CED connection:** Continuity: Indian Ocean networks (Swahili, Omani, Gujarati, Javanese merchants) persisted despite European disruption.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** Indian Ocean trade collapsed after Europeans opened Atlantic routes — Indian Ocean trade did not collapse after the opening of Atlantic routes.
- **(B)** The Ming navy monopolized Indian Ocean shipping throughout the period — The Ming scaled back maritime activity after Zheng He and did not monopolize the Indian Ocean.
- **(D)** European companies fully replaced Gujarati, Omani, and Javanese merchants by 1600 — The CED states that existing Asian merchant networks continued to flourish despite European arrival.

---

### Question 25: From Inca Mit'a to Colonial Mita — Answer (B)

**Correct answer:** (B) adapted an existing labor system to extract resources for a global market

**Explanation:** CED 4.4 states that colonial economies in the Americas used existing labor systems such as the Inca mit'a. The Inca mit'a was rotational labor for public works with state-provided food and security; Spain turned it into deadly forced labor mining silver for the world market. This CCOT question pairs institutional continuity with change in purpose and character.

**CED connection:** The Spanish mita co-opted an Inca rotational labor draft and turned it into deadly forced labor for silver extraction (CED 4.4).

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(A)** replaced indigenous labor in the mines with enslaved Africans — Indigenous mita laborers formed the core of the workforce at Potosí.
- **(C)** abolished indigenous labor obligations in favor of wage labor — The mita was not abolished; it was intensified and transformed.
- **(D)** preserved the reciprocal obligations that had characterized Inca rule — The Inca reciprocal obligations (state food storage and protection) disappeared, leaving only exploitative forced labor.

---

### Question 26: Indentured Servitude and Chattel Slavery — Answer (A)

**Correct answer:** (A) Indentured servitude ended after a set term, while chattel slavery was permanent and hereditary

**Explanation:** Indentured servants worked for a fixed term (usually 4–7 years) in exchange for passage and then gained freedom. Chattel slavery treated people as property that could be bought and sold, with status inherited by children. A basic concept, but frequently tested in comparison form on the AP exam.

**CED connection:** Indentured servitude = contract for fixed years in exchange for passage; chattel slavery = people as hereditary property.

**Why the other choices are wrong:**

- **(B)** Indentured servants were mostly Africans, while chattel slaves were mostly Europeans — The reverse is true: indentured servants were mostly Europeans and chattel slaves mostly Africans.
- **(C)** Chattel slavery existed only in Spanish America, while indentured servitude existed only in Brazil — Chattel slavery existed across Brazil, the Caribbean, and North America; indentured servitude was most common in British North America.
- **(D)** Indentured servants could be legally bought and sold as property for life — Being bought and sold as property for life is the definition of chattel slavery.

## Topic 4.5: Maritime Empires Maintained and Developed

### Question 27: The VOC Charter — Answer (B)

**Correct answer:** (B) The use of joint-stock companies to pool capital from many investors for overseas trade

**Explanation:** The VOC charter allowed anyone to invest as much as they wished, pooling capital from many investors. Joint-stock companies spread risk and profit among investors, and limited liability made investing safer. The free-trade option is a trap: the VOC was a state-chartered monopoly with a mercantilist character.
